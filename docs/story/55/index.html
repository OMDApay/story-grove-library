Question Tags

"You're coming to the party, aren't you?" asked Layla, as she and Mona walked home from school.

A little English

Question tags used systematically throughout as the central grammar teaching point ("aren't you?", "right?", "isn't it?", "don't you?", "didn't you?"); second conditional for hypothetical reflection ("if I said... it would sound"); reported dialogue with natural conversational flow; present perfect for accumulated observation ("I never noticed I was doing it").

"You're coming to the party, aren't you?" asked Layla, as she and Mona walked home from school. "I think so," said Mona. "Your mom said it starts at four, right?" "Yeah, but you should probably come a bit earlier, shouldn't you? So we can set up decorations together." "Good point," said Mona. "What time should I actually arrive?" "Maybe around three-thirty?" suggested Layla. "That gives us enough time, doesn't it?" "Perfect," said Mona. "Oh, and you want me to bring the balloons, don't you? You texted me yesterday about that." Layla laughed. "You remember everything, don't you? Yes, balloons would be great. But you don't have to pay for them all yourself, okay? We can split the cost." "That's fair," said Mona. "Your party's going to be amazing, isn't it? Twelve years old is a pretty big deal." "I guess," said Layla. "Though I'm kind of nervous about it, to be honest. What if nobody has fun?" Mona stopped walking and looked at her friend. "Layla, your parties are always fun, aren't they? You plan everything so carefully. Nobody's going to be bored, trust me." Layla smiled, feeling reassured. "Thanks, Mona. I don't know why I worry so much." "Because you care," said Mona simply. "That's not a bad thing, is it?" They continued walking, and soon they reached the corner where their paths split. "See you tomorrow, then?" asked Layla. "Definitely," said Mona. "Around three-thirty, right?" "Right," confirmed Layla. "And you know where I live, don't you? You've been to my house like a hundred times." Mona laughed. "I think I can find it, yeah. See you tomorrow!" As Layla walked the rest of the way home, she noticed how natural the conversation had felt. The question tags — those little confirmations at the end of sentences — made everything sound friendly and collaborative, not like she was demanding answers or making assumptions. That evening, she mentioned this to her older sister. "Mona and I were planning my party, and I kept saying things like 'right?' and 'isn't it?' and it didn't feel weird at all. It just felt... normal." "That's because it is normal," said her sister. "Question tags are how we check that we're on the same page without sounding pushy. Like, 'We're still friends, aren't we?' sounds way better than 'Are we still friends?' because it assumes we are, but just wants to confirm." Layla considered this. "So it's like a soft way of asking?" "Exactly," said her sister. "Instead of 'Did you finish the homework?' which sounds like you're checking up on someone, you say 'You finished the homework, didn't you?' which sounds like you trust they did, but just want to make sure." "That's actually really clever," said Layla. "I never noticed I was doing it before." "Most people don't," said her sister. "But it's one of those things that makes conversations flow smoothly. You should keep doing it." The following day, at the party, Layla noticed herself using question tags naturally throughout the afternoon. "This cake is delicious, isn't it?""We should play games soon, shouldn't we?""You're having fun, right?" Every time, the response was warm and engaged. Nobody seemed annoyed or pressured. Everyone just felt included in the conversation. By the end of the party, Layla's mother pulled her aside. "That went really well, didn't it?" she said, smiling. Layla laughed. "Yeah, it did, didn't it?" They both smiled, knowing that some of the most important communication skills weren't the ones explicitly taught — they were the ones organically absorbed through everyday conversation.
