The Personal Information Rule

When Omar got his first tablet, his parents sat down with him to set some rules.

A little English

Passive voice for rules and consequences ("should be kept," "should be shared," "can be copied"); reported speech for parent teaching ("his parents said personal information should stay private"); future forms for prediction ("could use it," "can be copied"); contrast clauses for nuanced explanation ("even if you delete it later"); question tags for gentle confirmation ("That's right, isn't it?").

When Omar got his first tablet, his parents sat down with him to set some rules. "Omar," said his father, "there are some things that should never be shared online. These are called personal information." "Like what?" asked Omar. "Like your full name, your address, your school name, and your phone number," explained his mother. "This information should be kept private because strangers could use it to find you or contact you." Omar nodded. "So I shouldn't tell anyone online my name?" "You can tell people your first name," said his father. "But your full name, your last name, and where you live — those should stay private." "What about photos?" asked Omar. "Photos should be shared carefully," said his mother. "And never with strangers. Once a photo is online, it can be copied and shared by other people — even if you delete it later." Omar thought about this. "So once something is online, it's like... permanent?" "Exactly," said his father. "That's why we say: think before you post. Ask yourself, 'Would I be okay if everyone saw this? My teacher, my friends, my grandparents?' If the answer is no, don't post it." The next day, Omar was playing a game online when another player asked him, "What's your real name? And what school do you go to?" Omar remembered his parents' rules. "I'm not supposed to share that information," he typed back. "Why not?" asked the player. "Because my parents said personal information should stay private," Omar explained. "It's not safe to share it with strangers online." The player didn't respond. Omar wasn't sure if he had done the right thing. That evening, he told his parents what had happened. "Did I do okay?" he asked. "You did perfectly," said his mother. "You protected your privacy. That's exactly what we want you to do." "And if someone asks again?" asked Omar. "Then you tell them again," said his father. "Or you stop talking to them and tell us. Your safety is more important than being polite to strangers." Omar nodded, feeling proud. He had learned an important rule: personal information stays private, always.
