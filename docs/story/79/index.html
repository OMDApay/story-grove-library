What They Said — Reported Speech + Relative Clauses

After school, Layla came home with a story to tell.

A little English

Reported speech used systematically ("Ms. Rania said that students who finish," "she reported that students who participate"); relative clauses used systematically ("students who finish," "the book that I borrowed," "the friend who sits," "the movie that we watched"); contrast clauses for conceptual distinction ("not just by studying it"); question tags for gentle confirmation ("That's right, isn't it?"); present perfect for accumulated realization ("you've realized").

After school, Layla came home with a story to tell. "Mom," she said, "Ms. Rania said something really interesting today. She said that students who finish their homework early can choose their seats for the next day." Her mother looked up from cooking. "So Ms. Rania said that students who finish early can choose their seats?" "Yes," said Layla. "But wait — you changed the words. Ms. Rania said, 'Students who finish their homework early can choose their seats.' But you said, 'Ms. Rania said that students who finish early can choose their seats.'" "That's right," said her mother. "I used reported speech to tell you what Ms. Rania said. And I also used a relative clause — 'who finish their homework early' — to describe which students can choose their seats." Layla thought about this. "So a relative clause is like... a clause that describes a noun?" "Exactly," said her mother. "In your sentence, 'students who finish their homework early,' the relative clause 'who finish their homework early' describes which students you're talking about." "Got it," said Layla. "So if I said, 'The book that I borrowed from the library is due tomorrow,' the relative clause 'that I borrowed from the library' describes which book?" "Precisely," said her mother. "You're using both reported speech and relative clauses correctly. That's advanced grammar for your age." That evening, Layla told her father about her day. "Ms. Rania said that students who participate in class get extra credit," she reported. "And she said that the project which is due next week is optional." Her father smiled. "You're using both structures perfectly," he said. "You reported what Ms. Rania said accurately, and you used relative clauses to describe which students and which project." Layla felt proud. She hadn't realized she was learning two grammar rules at once — but now that she knew, she noticed them everywhere. "The friend who sits next to me said that she's going to the park later," she told her father. "And the movie that we watched last night was really good." Her father laughed. "You're right," he said. "You're using both structures naturally now. That's how grammar works — you learn it by using it, not just by studying it."
