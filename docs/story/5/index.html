The Drawing Nobody Signed

Miss Farida pinned twenty-four drawings onto the classroom corkboard, one from every student in the class.

A little English

Simple past tense; past continuous ("was painting," "was thinking"); reported explanation; comparative and descriptive language; classroom dialogue with emotional escalation and resolution.

Miss Farida pinned twenty-four drawings onto the classroom corkboard, one from every student in the class. The assignment had been simple: draw your favorite place in the whole world. Noor's drawing showed a small fishing boat bobbing on turquoise water, exactly like the one her grandfather owned by the sea. She had spent all weekend on it, mixing blue and green paint until the water looked just right. When the drawings went up, the other children gathered around, pointing and laughing at their favorites. "Look at this castle! Whoever drew this is amazing!" said one boy, pointing at an elaborate pencil drawing. "This dinosaur one is so cool!" said a girl near the window. Nobody mentioned the fishing boat. Noor stood at the edge of the group, watching her classmates admire drawing after drawing, her own picture completely overlooked. Her chest felt tight and heavy. She slipped away to her desk and put her head down, blinking back tears she didn't want anyone to see. Miss Farida noticed and walked over quietly. "Noor? Everything alright?" "Nobody liked my drawing," Noor mumbled into her arms. "Maybe it's not good enough to talk about." Miss Farida pulled up a small chair beside her. "Can I ask you something? When you were painting that water, what were you thinking about?" Noor lifted her head slightly. "I was thinking about my grandfather. He takes me fishing every summer. The water by his dock looks exactly like that — that exact color, right where it gets deep." "Did painting it make you happy?" asked Miss Farida. "Yes," Noor admitted. "I loved doing it. I didn't even notice time passing." "Then it already succeeded," said Miss Farida. "A drawing's first job isn't to get compliments from classmates. Its first job is to say something true about how you see the world. You did that perfectly." "But nobody even looked at it," said Noor quietly. "Not yet," said Miss Farida, standing up. "Come with me." She led Noor back to the corkboard and pointed at the fishing boat picture. "Tell the class what you told me. About your grandfather, and the color of that water." Noor felt her face grow warm, but she took a breath and spoke to her classmates. "This is my grandfather's fishing dock. I go there every summer. The water turns this exact turquoise color where it gets deep, right before the boats go out." A boy named Tariq leaned closer to the painting. "Wait, that's real? I thought you just picked a pretty color." "It's exactly what it looks like," said Noor. "That's actually incredible," said a girl named Dina. "You can almost feel the water moving." Slowly, other classmates gathered around Noor's drawing, asking questions about her grandfather's boat and the sea. The tight feeling in Noor's chest melted away, replaced by something warmer. That evening, Noor told her grandfather about the whole day over the phone. "Nobody noticed my painting at first," she said, "but once I explained what it meant to me, everyone loved it." "Some of the best things in life," said her grandfather, "need a story behind them before people can truly see them. Never assume something isn't special just because nobody's noticed it yet."
