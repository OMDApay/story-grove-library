The Club Under the Oak Tree

A little English

Past continuous for background scene ("had started," "were whispering"); conditional worry structure ("What if they say no"); reported childhood memory nested in dialogue; present perfect for accumulated feeling ("I'd spent a whole month"); reflective closing dialogue generalizing the lesson.

At recess, four girls from Sena's class had started a "club" under the big oak tree, complete with secret handshakes and a rule that only members could sit on the special bench. Sena hovered nearby for the third day in a row, unsure how to ask if she could join, watching the girls whisper and giggle just out of earshot. "Why don't you just walk over and ask?" said her mother that evening, noticing Sena had been unusually quiet since school pickup. "What if they say no?" said Sena. "Then I'd know for sure they don't want me, instead of just wondering." Her mother sat down beside her on the bed. "I understand that fear. Not knowing feels safer than a clear no, even though it's actually worse in a different way — it keeps you stuck outside, wondering, every single day." "So what do I do?" asked Sena. "Can I tell you something that happened to me at your age?" said her mother. "There was a group of girls who always ate lunch together at one particular table. I wanted to join for an entire month before I finally asked." "What happened?" asked Sena eagerly. "They said sure, pull up a chair," said her mother, laughing at the memory. "That was it. No big speech, no rejection. I'd spent a whole month being anxious over something that took them two seconds to say yes to." "But what if my group says no?" Sena pressed. "Then you'll know something real instead of guessing forever," said her mother. "And here's something important: their answer isn't actually about whether you're likeable. Sometimes groups just aren't looking to add anyone, and that has nothing to do with you specifically." The next day at recess, Sena took a deep breath and walked toward the oak tree. "Hi," she said. "Your club looks fun. Is there room for one more person?" The girls exchanged glances. One of them, Maha, shrugged. "Sure, I guess. Do you know the handshake?" "Not yet," said Sena, relief flooding through her. "Can you teach me?" Within minutes, Sena was practicing the secret handshake, getting it wrong twice before finally getting it right, laughing along with the other girls at her own mistakes. That evening, Sena burst through the front door, grinning. "I'm in the club! I just asked, exactly like you said!" "How did it feel to ask?" her mother wondered. "Really scary right before," Sena admitted. "But it only took like ten seconds, and then it was over, and now I have a secret handshake." "That's usually how it goes," said her mother. "The wondering is almost always worse than the actual asking." "Next time I want something," said Sena thoughtfully, "I'm just going to ask instead of guessing forever." Her mother smiled. "That might be the single most useful thing you'll ever learn about making friends."
