The Question Nobody Had Asked

The school science fair was exactly one week away, and Salma still had no idea what to build.

A little English

Present perfect for experience ("I've always wondered," "nobody has ever taken"); past perfect for prior events ("had somehow found," "had already thought of"); superlatives ("the most interesting discoveries"); conditional/hypothetical reasoning; extended multi-day narrative sequencing with clear time markers.

The school science fair was exactly one week away, and Salma still had no idea what to build. Every other table already had a plan. Her best friend, Reem, was building a small volcano that erupted with fizzy foam. The twins from her class were constructing a model of the solar system with glowing planets. Even Khalid, who usually forgot his homework twice a week, had somehow found a kit to build a simple electric circuit. "What are you doing for your project?" Reem asked at lunch, stirring her soup absentmindedly. "I don't know," Salma admitted. "Everything good is already taken." "There's still a whole week," said Reem. "You'll think of something." But days passed, and nothing came to Salma. She flipped through science books at the library, read about volcanoes, circuits, plants, and magnets, yet nothing felt like hers. Everything seemed like something somebody else had already thought of first, and better. Three days before the fair, Salma sat in her backyard, frustrated and nearly ready to give up. She noticed her grandmother's small herb garden nearby, where mint, basil, and rosemary grew in neat little rows. "Grandma," Salma called out, "why does your mint grow so much faster than the basil?" Her grandmother, kneeling beside the plants with soil on her hands, looked up thoughtfully. "That's actually a wonderful question. I've always wondered the same thing myself. I water them exactly the same, and they get the same sunlight." Salma paused. "Wait... nobody actually knows the answer?" "Not that I've ever found," said her grandmother. "Perhaps it depends on the soil, or how much space each root needs, or something else entirely." An idea sparked in Salma's mind, small at first, then growing brighter. "Grandma, can I use your garden for my science project? I want to actually test it — different soil types, different amounts of water, and see which conditions make mint and basil grow fastest." Her grandmother's face lit up. "I would be delighted. Nobody has ever taken my garden mysteries so seriously before." For the next three days, Salma and her grandmother planted small identical cuttings of mint and basil in six different pots, each with slightly different soil and watering schedules. Salma measured each plant's height every morning and recorded the results carefully in a notebook, drawing simple charts to track the changes. By the morning of the science fair, Salma's pots showed clearly different growth patterns — one soil type had produced noticeably taller, healthier mint plants than the others. At the fair, Salma's table looked simple compared to Reem's foaming volcano and the twins' glowing solar system. But when the judges walked past, they stopped and asked real questions about her methods, her measurements, and her conclusions. "This is genuine scientific investigation," said one judge, examining her careful notebook of daily measurements. "You didn't just build something impressive-looking. You asked a real question and found real evidence." Salma won second place overall, right behind the twins' solar system. But what surprised her more was how many classmates lingered at her table afterward, asking about her grandmother's garden and her testing method. That evening, walking home with her ribbon, Salma told her grandmother everything. "I almost gave up because I thought I needed something flashy like a volcano," she said. "But the best idea was just a question nobody else had bothered to ask." Her grandmother squeezed her hand. "The most interesting discoveries rarely come from copying what everyone else is already doing. They come from paying attention to the ordinary questions right in front of you."
