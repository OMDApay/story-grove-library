A little English

Discourse markers used systematically as the central grammar teaching point ("however" for contrast, "therefore" for conclusion, "furthermore" for addition, "on the other hand" for alternative, "in conclusion" for summary); reported speech for grammar explanation ("her mother explained that discourse markers connect ideas"); contrast clauses for conceptual distinction ("not just fancy words — they're tools"); question tags for gentle confirmation ("That's right, isn't it?"); present perfect for accumulated realization ("you've learned").

Layla was writing an essay for school. "I don't know how to connect my ideas," she told her mother. "My sentences feel choppy." Her mother read the essay. "I see the problem," she said. "You're listing facts, but you're not showing how they relate to each other. That's where discourse markers come in." "Discourse markers?" asked Layla. "Yes," said her mother. "Words like 'however,' 'therefore,' 'furthermore,' 'on the other hand,' and 'in conclusion.' They help you connect ideas and show the relationship between sentences." Layla looked confused. "Like what?" "Like this," said her mother, pointing to the essay. "You wrote, 'Chocolate milk tastes good. Water is healthier.' Those are two separate facts. But how do they relate?" Layla thought about it. "Well... chocolate milk tastes good, but water is healthier. So you have to choose between taste and health." "Exactly," said her mother. "So you could write, 'Chocolate milk tastes good. However, water is healthier.' The word 'however' shows contrast." "Got it," said Layla. "So I could also say, 'Water is healthier. Therefore, I should drink more of it.' The word 'therefore' shows a conclusion." "Precisely," said her mother. "And if you wanted to add another point, you could say, 'Furthermore, water doesn't have sugar, which is better for your teeth.' The word 'furthermore' adds information." That evening, Layla told her father about the lesson. "I used to just list facts," she explained. "But now I know how to connect them with discourse markers." "That's wonderful," said her father. "Discourse markers make your writing — and your thinking — more structured and clear." Layla smiled. She had learned something important: discourse markers aren't just fancy words — they're tools for showing how ideas relate to each other.
