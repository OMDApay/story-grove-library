The Decision to Try

Layla was trying to decide what to do after school.

A little English

Gerunds and infinitives used systematically as the central grammar teaching point ("want to play" — infinitive, "avoiding my homework" — gerund, "enjoy reading" — gerund, "decided to read" — infinitive); reported speech for multi-turn dialogue ("she reported that she decided to finish"); contrast clauses for conceptual distinction ("some verbs are followed by infinitives... and some by gerunds"); question tags for gentle confirmation ("That's right, isn't it?"); present perfect for accumulated experience ("you've been enjoying").

Layla was trying to decide what to do after school. "I want to play outside," she told her mother. "But I also want to finish my book. And I've been avoiding my homework all week." Her mother smiled. "You're using gerunds and infinitives correctly," she observed. "You said 'I want to play' — that's an infinitive. And you said 'I've been avoiding my homework' — that's a gerund." Layla looked confused. "What's the difference?" "Some verbs are followed by infinitives," explained her mother. "Like 'want to play,' 'decide to go,' 'plan to visit.' And some verbs are followed by gerunds — the -ing form. Like 'enjoy playing,' 'avoid doing,' 'consider trying.'" "Got it," said Layla. "So I could say, 'I enjoy reading,' or 'I decided to read'?" "Exactly," said her mother. "But you couldn't say, 'I enjoy to read' or 'I decided reading.' Those don't sound right." Layla thought about this. "So which verbs take gerunds, and which take infinitives?" "There are patterns," said her mother. "Verbs like 'enjoy,' 'avoid,' 'consider,' and 'practice' usually take gerunds. And verbs like 'want,' 'decide,' 'plan,' and 'hope' usually take infinitives." "That's a lot to remember," said Layla. "It is," agreed her mother. "But you're already using them correctly in your speech. You just didn't know the grammar names for them." That evening, Layla told her father about her day. "I decided to finish my homework first," she reported. "And I've been enjoying my book, so I plan to read it after homework." Her father smiled. "You're using both patterns perfectly," he said. "'Decided to finish' — that's an infinitive. And 'enjoying my book' — that's a gerund." Layla felt proud. She hadn't realized she was using two grammar structures at once — but now that she knew, she noticed them everywhere. "I want to keep practicing," she told her father. "And I enjoy learning new things." Her father laughed. "That's perfect," he said. "'Want to keep' — infinitive. 'Enjoy learning' — gerund. You've got it."

English verbs follow patterns — some (like enjoy, avoid, consider, practice) are followed by gerunds (-ing form), and others (like want, decide, plan, hope) are followed by infinitives (to + verb); learning these patterns helps us speak and write more naturally, and we're often using them correctly before we know the grammar names.
