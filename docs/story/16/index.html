The Hug Mira Didn't Want

At the family gathering, Uncle Fadi's friend, a man Mira had never met before, kept trying to sit her on his lap.

A little English

Present tense for rules and general truths ("your body always belongs to you"); modal "should" for advice; conditional structure ("If someone ever makes you feel..."); reported classroom dialogue; imperative structure for safety instructions.

At the family gathering, Uncle Fadi's friend, a man Mira had never met before, kept trying to sit her on his lap. "Come here, sweetheart, give me a hug," the man said, reaching for Mira's arm with a wide smile. Mira stepped back instead, standing closer to her mother's side. Something about the way he smiled made her stomach feel tight and uncomfortable, even though she couldn't explain exactly why. "Don't be shy," the man laughed, reaching again. "It's just a hug." "I don't want to," Mira said quietly, gripping her mother's hand tighter. Her mother noticed immediately and stepped slightly in front of Mira. "She doesn't have to hug anyone she doesn't want to, even at a family party. That's completely her choice." The man shrugged, looking a little annoyed, and walked away to talk to someone else. Later that evening, in the car ride home, Mira's mother turned around from the front seat. "I noticed you didn't want a hug from Uncle Fadi's friend today. I want you to know something important: you never have to let anyone hug you, kiss you, or touch you if it makes you feel uncomfortable. Not family friends, not relatives, not even people we know well. Your body always belongs to you." "Even if they get upset?" asked Mira. "Even then," said her mother firmly. "A grown-up's feelings are never more important than your comfort and safety. If someone ever makes you feel that tight, uncomfortable feeling in your stomach — the one you felt tonight — that feeling is important information. It's your body telling you something isn't right." "What should I do if that happens again?" asked Mira. "Say no, clearly and loudly if you need to," said her mother. "Walk away toward me, your father, a teacher, or any grown-up you trust. And always, always tell me afterward, no matter what anyone says about keeping it a secret. Some secrets are fun, like surprise parties. But secrets about anyone touching you, or asking you to keep touching a secret, are never okay to keep. Those secrets, you tell a trusted grown-up immediately." Mira thought about this carefully. "What if it's someone I really like, like a favorite teacher or a family member?" "The rule stays exactly the same for everyone, no matter who they are," said her mother. "The parts of your body covered by your swimsuit are private. Nobody has a good reason to ask you to keep secrets about them, or to touch them, except a doctor checking if you're healthy, and even then, usually with me or your father right there in the room." A week later, at school, Mira's teacher spent one lesson talking about exactly the same rules — private body parts, trusted adults, and the difference between secrets and surprises. Mira raised her hand confidently. "My mom already taught me this," she said. "You always tell someone you trust. Every single time." Her teacher smiled warmly. "That's exactly right, Mira. And the more grown-ups who remind you, the safer and more confident you'll feel." That night, Mira told her mother about the lesson at school. "It's good that they teach it there too," her mother said, hugging her gently, only because Mira leaned in first. "The most important rule of all is this: you are always allowed to say no, and you should always tell someone you trust. Every time, no exceptions."
