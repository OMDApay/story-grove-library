The Time Estimation Game

On Saturday morning, Omar's mother introduced a new challenge.

A little English

Future forms for prediction ("you'll estimate," "it will take"); reported speech for parent teaching ("his mother explained that estimating time is a skill"); contrast clauses for nuanced explanation ("but he didn't feel frustrated"); question tags for gentle confirmation ("That's right, isn't it?"); present perfect for accumulated improvement ("you've got a plan").

On Saturday morning, Omar's mother introduced a new challenge. "Omar," she said, "before you start any task today, I want you to guess how long it will take. Then we'll time it and see how close you were." Omar looked confused. "Why?" "Because estimating time is a skill," explained his mother. "And when you get better at it, you can plan your day more effectively." Omar considered this. "Okay. So how long do you think cleaning my room will take?" His mother smiled. "I'm not going to tell you. I want you to guess first." Omar thought about it. "Maybe... thirty minutes?" "Great," said his mother. "Now let's time it and see." Omar started cleaning. He picked up his clothes, made his bed, and put his toys away. When he finished, he checked the timer. "Forty-five minutes," he said, surprised. "I was way off." "That's okay," said his mother. "Now you know. Next time, you'll estimate more accurately." That afternoon, Omar had homework, chores, and free time to fit into his schedule. "Let's prioritize," suggested his mother. "What must you do today? What should you do? And what could you do if there's time?" Omar thought about it. "I must do my homework. I should clean my room. And I could play video games if I finish early." "Perfect," said his mother. "Now you've got a plan." Omar started with his homework — the must-do task. He estimated it would take thirty minutes. It took forty. But he didn't feel frustrated — he was learning. Next came his room — the should-do task. He estimated forty-five minutes this time. It took forty-five. He was getting better. Finally, he had free time — the could-do reward. He played video games for an hour, feeling proud of what he had accomplished. That evening, Omar told his father about the day. "I learned that I'm not great at estimating time yet," he admitted. "But I'm getting better." "That's exactly how skills work," said his father. "You practice, you adjust, and you improve. Time management is a skill you'll use your whole life." Omar nodded. He had learned something important: estimating time isn't about being perfect — it's about getting better with practice.
