The Girl Who Couldn't Sit Still

A little English

Present perfect for ongoing teacher observation ("I've been observing," "I've started letting"); passive-adjacent explanatory structure for developmental concepts; conditional collaborative proposal ("would you like to try"); comparative reasoning about individual development rates; resolution dialogue reframing "problem" as "information."

"Deema, please stay in your seat," Mr. Waleed said for the third time that morning, watching her wander to the classroom window yet again during independent reading time. "Sorry, Mr. Waleed," said Deema, sliding back into her chair, only to be standing by the bookshelf four minutes later, seemingly without noticing she'd moved at all. At the parent-teacher meeting that week, Deema's father looked concerned. "Is she being disruptive? She never used to have trouble sitting still at home." Mr. Waleed considered this carefully before answering. "She's not disruptive exactly. She's just... constantly moving. Standing, sitting, wandering a few steps, sitting again. I've been observing the pattern rather than just correcting it every time." "What have you noticed?" asked Deema's father. "She actually listens perfectly well while moving," said Mr. Waleed. "Her reading comprehension is excellent. It's specifically sitting still for long stretches that seems difficult for her, not focusing itself." "So what does that mean?" asked her father, genuinely wanting to understand rather than simply fix the problem. "Children at this age are still developing what's called gross motor control," explained Mr. Waleed. "Some children's bodies need more movement than others to feel regulated and comfortable, especially during long periods of sitting. It's a completely normal part of physical development, not misbehavior." "Should we be worried?" asked her father. "Not at all," said Mr. Waleed. "But I do think we can work with it instead of constantly fighting against it. I've started letting a few students use a standing desk option or take short movement breaks between activities. Would you like to try something similar at home too?" That evening, Deema's father set up a small area in the living room with a wobble cushion for her desk chair, something he'd read about that allowed gentle movement while sitting. The next week, Mr. Waleed tried something new during independent reading time. Instead of asking Deema to stay perfectly still, he gave her permission to stand at the back of the classroom with her book whenever she needed to, as long as she stayed quiet and didn't disturb anyone else. Deema stood for a few minutes, then sat, then stood again, moving freely between the two options throughout the reading period. For the first time all year, she finished an entire chapter without once being reminded to return to her seat. "How did that feel?" Mr. Waleed asked her afterward. "Really good," said Deema. "I didn't have to keep thinking about sitting still. I could just think about the story instead." Two weeks later, at pickup time, Deema's father asked how she was doing. "She's doing wonderfully," said Mr. Waleed. "Once we stopped treating the movement as a problem to eliminate and started treating it as information about how her body learns best, everything got easier for both of us." Deema's father smiled with relief. "I always assumed she just wasn't trying hard enough to sit still. I never pondered that her body might actually need to move in order to focus properly." "Most people assume that at first," said Mr. Waleed. "But bodies develop at different rates, in different ways. Deema's isn't wrong. It just needs a little more room to move than some of her classmates."
