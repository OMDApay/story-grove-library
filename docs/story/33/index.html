A Group of Three

For an entire year, Jana and Reem had been inseparable best friends, sharing lunch every single day and finishing each other's sentences.

A little English

Past perfect continuous for established friendship history ("had been inseparable"); present perfect for recent noticed change ("have been sitting"); conditional advice structure ("you could try"); modeled "I-statement" dialogue construction; resolution dialogue reframing conflict as natural change rather than betrayal.

For an entire year, Jana and Reem had been inseparable best friends, sharing lunch every single day and finishing each other's sentences. Then Noor joined their class in September. By October, Jana noticed something shifting. Reem and Noor had started sitting together at the far end of the lunch table, whispering about things Jana wasn't part of. Twice that week, Jana arrived at their usual meeting spot only to find Reem already gone, off somewhere with Noor instead. "Are you mad at me?" Jana finally asked Reem after school on Thursday. "No, why would I be mad?" said Reem, sounding genuinely confused. "You've been sitting with Noor a lot," said Jana. "It feels like you're choosing her instead of me." Reem paused. "I didn't realize it felt that way. I like Noor, but I'm not choosing her over you. Can't I have two friends?" Jana hadn't considered that possibility. "I guess I just assumed friendship only worked with two people at a time, like it's always been with us." That evening, Jana told her father about the whole confusing situation. "It's not really fighting," she said, "but it doesn't feel good either. I don't know what to call it." Her father listened carefully. "It sounds like your friendship is changing shape, not ending. That's actually really different from a real conflict, even though it can feel just as uncomfortable." "How is it different?" asked Jana. "In a real conflict, someone usually did something clearly hurtful on purpose," said her father. "This sounds more like Reem simply made a new friend, and your friendship now has to stretch to include a third person. That stretching feeling is uncomfortable, but it's not the same as betrayal." "So what do I do?" asked Jana. "You could try using an 'I' statement instead of assuming the worst," said her father. "Something like, 'I felt a little left out this week when you sat with Noor without me.' That's different from accusing her of choosing sides." The next day, Jana found Reem before lunch. "I felt kind of left out this week," she said carefully, using exactly the words her father suggested. "I wasn't sure if you still wanted to be friends with me." Reem's face softened immediately. "I definitely still want to be friends with you. I didn't realize I was making you feel that way. Want to all sit together today? I think you and Noor would actually get along really well." At lunch, the three of them sat together for the first time, and within twenty minutes, Jana discovered that Noor loved the exact same book series she did, something Reem hadn't even known about either. By the following week, lunch had become a comfortable trio instead of a confusing puzzle, with occasional days where two of them paired off for something specific, and everyone understanding that this didn't mean anyone was being excluded. That evening, Jana told her father how it turned out. "We're a group of three now," she said. "It felt weird at first, but it's actually kind of nice having two best friends instead of worrying about losing one." "That's a really mature way to see it," said her father. "Friendships changing shape doesn't have to mean anyone loses anything."
