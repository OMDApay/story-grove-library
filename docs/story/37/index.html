The Corner Shop

"Just take one piece of gum," whispered Tariq's friend Bassam, glancing toward the unattended shop counter.

A little English

First conditional reinforced ("If we'd actually done it and gotten caught, that would've been"); reported internal conflict narration; modal reasoning about social risk (might think, could ruin); comparative reasoning about friendship quality; resolution dialogue distinguishing momentary annoyance from genuine respect.

"Just take one piece of gum," whispered Tariq's friend Bassam, glancing toward the unattended shop counter. "The owner's in the back room. Nobody will ever notice." Tariq's stomach tightened. They'd walked to the corner shop together after school, like they did most Thursdays, but Bassam had never suggested anything like this before. "That's stealing," Tariq said quietly. "It's literally one piece of gum," said Bassam, rolling his eyes. "It costs practically nothing. You're being so dramatic about it." "I don't care how much it costs," said Tariq, though his voice quivered slightly. "It's still not mine." "Everyone does stuff like this sometimes," said Bassam. "It's honestly not a big deal. Are you scared, or something?" Tariq felt his face grow hot. Part of him worried that saying no again would make Bassam think he was boring, or scared, or not worth hanging out with anymore. But a stronger part of him kept picturing the shop owner, Mr. Adly, who always greeted them by name and sometimes gave them a free sticker with their purchases. "I'm not scared," said Tariq, surprising himself with how steady his voice sounded. "I just don't want to do it. You can if you want, but I'm not going to." Bassam hesitated, glancing at the gum, then at Tariq, then back at the gum. For a long moment, neither boy moved. Finally, Bassam shoved his hands into his pockets. "Fine, whatever. It wasn't that important anyway." They left the shop together, an odd silence hanging between them the entire walk home. Tariq worried he'd ruined the friendship over something so small. The next day at school, Bassam sat with him at lunch like usual, seemingly unbothered, as though nothing unusual had happened at all. "Hey," said Bassam eventually, not quite meeting Tariq's eyes. "About yesterday. You were right. I don't even know why I said that. My older cousin does stuff like that sometimes, and I guess I thought it sounded cool." "It's okay," said Tariq. "I was worried you'd be mad at me for saying no." "Honestly, I was a little annoyed for like five minutes," Bassam admitted. "But mostly I think I was relieved you said no. If we'd actually done it and gotten caught, that would've been so much worse." That evening, Tariq told his older sister about the whole situation. "I almost said yes," he admitted openly, "just because I didn't want him to think I was boring." His sister nodded knowingly. "That happens to basically everyone at some point. The tricky part isn't knowing right from wrong. It's staying firm on it even when someone you like is pushing you the other way." "How do you get better at that?" asked Tariq. "Practice, mostly," said his sister. "And realizing that a friend who only likes you when you do risky things probably isn't as good a friend as one who respects you saying no." The following Thursday, Tariq and Bassam walked past the same shop again, waving hello to Mr. Adly through the window, and continued on toward home exactly like every typical Thursday before.
