The Comment That Hurt

When Sara saw the mean comment on her friend's photo, she wasn't sure what to do.

A little English

Passive voice for consequences ("might be removed," "wasn't sure what to do"); reported speech for mother's teaching ("her mother had taught her that being kind online is important"); contrast clauses for choice framing ("you can add to the problem, or you can add to the solution"); question tags for gentle confirmation ("That's right, isn't it?"); present perfect for accumulated learning ("you've learned").

When Sara saw the mean comment on her friend's photo, she wasn't sure what to do. The comment said, "This picture is ugly. You look weird." Sara knew it wasn't kind. But she also knew she wasn't the one who had posted it. "Should I say something?" she asked herself. "Or should I just ignore it?" She thought about what her mother had taught her: "Being kind online is just as important as being kind in person. And if you see something mean, you have a choice: you can add to the problem, or you can add to the solution." Sara took a deep breath and typed a comment of her own. "I think this photo is beautiful," she wrote. "You look happy, and that's what matters." A few minutes later, her friend replied. "Thank you, Sara. That mean comment made me feel really bad. But your comment made me feel better." Sara felt relieved. She hadn't stopped the mean comment — but she had added something kind to balance it out. That evening, she told her mother what had happened. "I didn't know what to do," she admitted. "But I remembered what you said about adding to the solution." Her mother pulled her into a hug. "That's called digital citizenship," she explained. "It means being responsible and kind online, just like you are in person." "But what if the mean comment stays there?" asked Sara. "What if other people see it?" "Then you can report it," said her mother. "Most apps and websites have a way to report mean or hurtful comments. The comment might be removed, and the person who posted it might learn that their words have consequences." Sara nodded. "So being a good digital citizen means... being kind, reporting mean stuff, and thinking before I post?" "Exactly," said her mother. "And remembering that everything you post online leaves a digital footprint — a record of what you've done. You want your footprint to show kindness, not meanness." Sara smiled. She had learned something important: being kind online wasn't just about following rules. It was about being the kind of person who adds good to the world, wherever she goes — online or offline.
