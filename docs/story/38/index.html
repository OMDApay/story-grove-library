The If-Then Plan

"I want to read twenty whole books by the end of the school year," Hind announced at dinner, holding up a list she'd copied from the school library's reading challenge poster.

A little English

First conditional used systematically and repeatedly as a planning tool ("if it's... then I'll..."); present perfect for accumulated progress ("had finished nineteen books"); comparative structure for growth over time ("four times as many"); reflective dialogue distinguishing a "wish" from a "plan."

"I want to read twenty whole books by the end of the school year," Hind announced at dinner, holding up a list she'd copied from the school library's reading challenge poster. "That's a wonderful goal," said her father. "How many books have you read so far this year?" Hind checked her list. "Three. But there's still eight months left." "Twenty books sounds achievable," said her father, "but goals work much better with an actual plan attached. Right now, you just have a wish. Let's turn it into something more concrete." "What's the difference?" asked Hind. "A wish is 'I want to read twenty books.' A plan is knowing exactly when and how you'll do it," said her father. "For example: if it's after dinner on a school night, then you'll read for fifteen minutes before starting homework. If it's a weekend morning, then you'll read for thirty minutes before turning on any screens." Hind considered this. "So I make a rule for different situations?" "Exactly," said her father. "It's called an if-then plan. Instead of hoping you'll magically remember to read every day, you decide in advance exactly what triggers your reading time." They wrote out Hind's plan together: If it's a school night after dinner, then I read for fifteen minutes before homework. If it's Saturday morning, then I read for thirty minutes before breakfast. If I finish a book, then I immediately choose the next one so there's no gap. For the first two weeks, Hind followed her plan faithfully, finishing two more books. But during the third week, a busy schedule of birthday parties and family visits completely disrupted her routine, and she read nothing for five straight days. "I already messed up my plan," Hind told her father, discouraged. "Maybe twenty books isn't actually possible." "One difficult week doesn't erase the plan," said her father. "What actually happened this week that made it hard?" Hind thought about it. "We had somewhere to be almost every single evening. There wasn't really a normal 'after dinner' time at all." "So maybe the plan needs one more rule," suggested her father. "What if we added: if the evening is unusually chaotic, then I read for even just five minutes before falling asleep, no matter how tired I am?" "That seems like it wouldn't really count," said Hind. "Five minutes of reading during a hectic week counts for a lot more than zero minutes," said her father. "The goal isn't perfection. It's consistency, even in a smaller form, during hard weeks." Hind added the new rule to her list. Over the following months, her plan flexed and adjusted several times — sometimes ten minutes became five, sometimes an extra book got squeezed in during a quiet weekend. By the end of the school year, Hind had finished nineteen books, one short of her original goal of twenty. "I didn't actually make it," she told her father, slightly disappointed. "You read nineteen books this year," said her father. "How many did you read last year, before we made any plan at all?" Hind thought back. "Maybe four or five." "So this year, with a flexible plan instead of just a wish, you read almost four times as many books," said her father. "That's not falling short. That's a substantial success, even if the exact number wasn't twenty." Hind smiled, looking at her stack of nineteen finished books on the shelf. "Next year, I'm making the plan again, right from the start."
