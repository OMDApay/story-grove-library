The New School

In two weeks, Layla would be starting at a completely new school, and she couldn't decide whether she felt more excited or terrified.

A little English

Second conditional for hypothetical worry ("what if nobody likes me?"); future continuous for upcoming events ("would be starting"); adverbial clauses of contrast ("even though you were nervous"); reported reassurance dialogue with validation; question tags for gentle confirmation ("That's okay, isn't it?").

In two weeks, Layla would be starting at a completely new school, and she couldn't decide whether she felt more excited or terrified. "I don't want to leave my friends," she told her mother one evening, halfway through packing a box of her books. "What if nobody at the new school likes me?" Her mother sat down on the floor beside the half-packed box. "That's a really normal thing to worry about. Starting over is hard, even for adults." "But you don't have to start over," said Layla. "You already know people. I'll be the new kid who doesn't know anyone." "That's true," admitted her mother. "But you know what? The new school already has a plan for kids like you. They're going to assign you a buddy — someone in your class who'll show you around, sit with you at lunch, and introduce you to other kids during the first few weeks." Layla looked up, surprised. "They do that?" "They do," said her mother. "And your teacher has already been told you're coming, so she'll be expecting you. You won't just be walking into a room full of strangers." Layla considered this. "What if my buddy is mean, though? What if they think I'm weird?" "Then that's information about them, not about you," said her mother simply. "But here's what usually happens: most kids are actually curious about new students, not judgmental. They want to know where you came from, what you like, whether you play any games they play." Layla picked up another book and placed it carefully in the box. "I'm still nervous." "I know you are," said her mother. "And that's okay. You can be nervous and excited at the same time. Both feelings are valid." The following week, Layla's mother arranged a visit to the new school before the first day. They walked the hallways together, found the classroom, located the bathrooms and cafeteria, and even met the principal briefly. "This is where you'll line up on the first morning," said her mother, pointing to a spot near the entrance. "And that's your classroom, right there. See? You already know where things are." Layla felt a small knot of tension loosen slightly in her chest. "It's smaller than I imagined." "That's usually how it is," said her mother. "Things seem bigger in our heads than they actually are." On the first day of school, Layla's mother walked her to the entrance, where a teacher was waiting with a small group of new students. "Good morning," said the teacher warmly. "I'm Ms. Rania, and I'll be showing you around today. Layla, you're with me, remember? We talked on the phone last week." Layla nodded, feeling a small surge of relief. At least one person already knew her name. As the day unfolded, Layla discovered that her buddy, a girl named Hana, was friendly and chatty, asking heartfelt questions about Layla's old school and whether she liked art or sports more. By lunch time, Layla had already been introduced to three other kids, and Hana had saved her a seat at a table near the window. That evening, Layla told her mother about the whole day. "It wasn't as bad as I thought," she admitted. "Hana was really nice, and I actually recognized some places from our visit." "I'm so glad," said her mother. "How are you feeling now?" Layla thought about it. "Still a little nervous, I guess. But also... kind of hopeful? Like maybe this could actually work out okay." "That's exactly right," said her mother. "Both feelings can exist at the same time. And you know what? You handled today really well, even though you were nervous. That's something to be proud of." Layla smiled slightly. "Yeah. I guess I did."
