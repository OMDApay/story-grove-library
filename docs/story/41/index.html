Not Yet Fast Enough

The list was posted on the gym door: eleven names for the school swim team.

A little English

Past perfect for prior effort ("had practiced every single afternoon," "had expected"); modals of possibility (could try out again, could ask, could decide); comparative structure ("faster than I've ever been... still isn't fast enough"); resolution dialogue reframing "I can't" as "not yet."

The list was posted on the gym door: eleven names for the school swim team. Yusuf's name wasn't among them. He read it three times, certain he'd somehow missed his own name, before the truth settled heavily in his chest. He'd practiced every single afternoon for a month, timing his laps with his father's old stopwatch, and it still hadn't been enough. "It's okay," said his friend Adel, patting his shoulder. "There's always next year." Something about the words made Yusuf's throat tighten uncomfortably. It wasn't okay. Not yet, anyway. At home, his mother noticed immediately. "How did tryouts go?" "I didn't make it," Yusuf mumbled, dropping his swim bag by the door. "Oh, Yusuf," said his mother, crouching down instead of immediately offering solutions. "That's really disappointing. You worked incredibly hard for that." Yusuf blinked, surprised. He'd expected her to jump straight to something encouraging, the way Adel had. "You're not going to say it's okay?" he asked. "It's not okay, actually," said his mother. "It's disappointing, and you're allowed to feel exactly that, without anyone rushing you past it. You practiced every day for a month. Missing the team after that effort would sting for anyone." Yusuf felt something loosen slightly in his chest, as though being allowed to feel bad made the feeling somehow more bearable than being told not to feel it. "I really thought I'd make it," he admitted, his eyes stinging. "I was faster than I've ever been." "You probably were," said his mother. "Sometimes being faster than you've ever been still isn't fast enough compared to everyone else trying out. That's genuinely hard, and it has nothing to do with whether your effort mattered." They sat together quietly for a while, Yusuf's disappointment sitting openly between them instead of being brushed aside. Later that evening, once the sharpest sting had faded, his mother asked gently, "Would it help to talk about what happens next, or do you need more time first?" "I think I'm ready," said Yusuf, wiping his eyes. "What happens next?" "That depends on you," said his mother. "You could try out again next year with another year of practice behind you. You could ask the coach what specifically held you back this time. Or you could decide swimming competitively isn't actually what you want, and that's a real option too." Yusuf considered this. "Could I ask the coach what I need to work on? I don't want to just guess." "That sounds like a really sensible idea," said his mother. The next day, Yusuf approached his coach nervously. "Could you tell me what I need to improve for next year's tryouts?" His coach looked surprised, then pleased. "Your turns lost you almost two full seconds each lap. Your straight-line speed was actually competitive. Fix the turns, and you'd have made the team easily." That evening, Yusuf told his mother everything. "I'm not there yet," he said, "but now I actually know what 'not yet' means for me specifically." "That's exactly the right way to think about it," said his mother. "Not 'I can't swim fast enough.' Just 'not yet, and now I know why.'"
