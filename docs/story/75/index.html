The Friend Who Moved Away — Relative Clauses

Layla's best friend, Sara, was moving to a different city.

A little English

Relative clauses used systematically as the central grammar teaching point ("the friend who understands me," "the photo that we took," "the giraffes that we fed," "the day when we laughed"); reported speech for multi-turn dialogue ("she said we were the kind of friends who can stay close"); contrast clauses for nuanced emotion ("even though she felt like crying"); question tags for gentle confirmation ("That's wonderful, isn't it?"); present perfect for accumulated experience ("you've received").

Layla's best friend, Sara, was moving to a different city. "I don't want you to go," Layla said sadly, sitting on Sara's bedroom floor as Sara packed her toys into boxes. "I don't want to go either," said Sara. "But my dad got a new job that requires him to move." Layla picked up a framed photo of the two of them. "This is the photo that we took at the zoo last year," she said. "Remember the giraffes that we fed?" Sara smiled. "I remember. That was the day when we laughed so hard at the monkey that ate my sandwich." Layla laughed, even though she felt like crying. "You're the friend who always makes me laugh," she said. "Even when I'm sad." Sara hugged her. "You're the friend who listens when I need to talk. That's the kind of friend that I'll never forget." That evening, Layla told her mother about the day. "Sara is the friend who understands me best," she explained. "And now she's moving to a city that's two hours away." Her mother listened carefully. "It sounds like you're really sad about losing a friend who matters so much to you." Layla nodded. "But Sara said we're the kind of friends who can stay close even when we're far apart. She said we'll video call every week, and that's the thing that makes me feel a little better." "That's wonderful," said her mother. "Distance changes how you see each other, but it doesn't have to change who you are to each other." The next week, after Sara had moved, Layla received a package in the mail. Inside was a photo album that Sara had made, filled with pictures of all the adventures that they had shared. On the first page, Sara had written: "You are the friend who made my childhood special. Distance is just a number that separates our houses, not our hearts." Layla smiled through tears. Sara was right. They were the kind of friends who would stay connected, no matter which city either of them lived in.

Relative clauses help us describe people and things precisely — "the friend who listens," "the photo that we took," "the day when we laughed" — making our communication clearer and more specific; distance can separate houses, but not hearts, when friendship is real.
