The Three Jars

When Adam got his first allowance, his father gave him three jars.

A little English

Future forms for prediction ("you won't have," "you can buy"); reported speech for parent teaching ("his father explained that money has three purposes"); contrast clauses for nuanced explanation ("but if you spend everything now"); question tags for gentle confirmation ("That's right, isn't it?"); present perfect for accumulated understanding ("you've learned").

When Adam got his first allowance, his father gave him three jars. "This jar," he said, pointing to the first, "is for saving. This jar is for spending. And this jar is for sharing." Adam looked confused. "Why three jars?" "Because money has three purposes," explained his father. "Saving is for future goals — like a toy you want next month. Spending is for things you want now — like a snack after school. And sharing is for helping others — like donating to a charity or buying a gift for someone." Adam considered this. "So how much goes in each jar?" "That's up to you," said his father. "But a common rule is: half for saving, a quarter for spending, and a quarter for sharing." Adam thought about it. "But what if I want to buy something now?" "Then you can use your spending jar," said his father. "But if you spend everything now, you won't have anything saved for later. That's called delayed gratification — waiting for something better instead of taking what's available now." Adam nodded slowly. "So if I save my money, I can buy something bigger later?" "Exactly," said his father. "That's the power of saving. It lets you trade small wants now for bigger goals later." That week, Adam got his allowance. He put half in the saving jar, a quarter in the spending jar, and a quarter in the sharing jar. At the store, he saw a candy bar he wanted. He could buy it with his spending money — but then he'd have nothing left for the week. He thought about it. "I'll wait," he told his father. "I want to save for the bigger toy." His father smiled. "That's delayed gratification," he said. "And it's a skill that will help you your whole life." Adam felt proud. He had learned something important: money isn't just about buying things now — it's about making choices that align with your goals.
