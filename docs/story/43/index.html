Two Holidays

A little English

Present tense for explaining cultural practices and traditions; present perfect for accumulated tradition ("has been listening"); comparative structure ("way better than regular cookies"); reported classroom dialogue integrating factual explanation; extended time sequencing across two winters.

"Why don't you have a Christmas tree at your house?" asked Lucy during the classroom holiday craft session, watching Fatima carefully cut out a paper star instead of the snowman everyone else was making. "We don't celebrate Christmas," said Fatima. "My family celebrates Eid instead. It's a different holiday, at a different time of year." "What's Eid?" asked Lucy, genuinely curious rather than unkind. Fatima considered how to explain it. "There are actually two Eids. One comes after a whole month of fasting called Ramadan, where we don't eat or drink anything from sunrise to sunset. The other one happens after a special pilgrimage some people take." "You don't eat ANYTHING all day?" asked Lucy, eyes wide. "That sounds impossible." "It feels hard the first few days," admitted Fatima, "but you kind of get used to it. And when the sun goes down, my whole family eats together, and it's honestly one of my favorite parts of the entire year." "What do you do on Eid itself?" asked Lucy. "We wear new clothes, visit all our relatives, and the grown-ups give money to the kids," said Fatima, smiling at the memory. "My grandmother makes this pastry with dates inside called maamoul. I look forward to it basically all year." Their teacher, Mrs. Peterson, had been listening nearby. "Fatima, would you like to bring in some maamoul next month, near when Eid happens this year? We could learn about it together as a class, the same way we're learning about Christmas traditions today." Fatima's face lit up. "Really? I could ask my grandmother to help me make some." The following month, Fatima arrived at school with a container of homemade maamoul, and her mother came in briefly to help explain the holiday to the class using a few photographs from previous celebrations. "These cookies are amazing," said Lucy, taking a second one without being offered. "Way better than regular cookies." "Thank you," said Fatima, beaming with pride. "My grandmother's recipe is honestly the best one in our whole family." After the presentation, Lucy approached Fatima at recess. "I never really thought about how not everyone celebrates the same holidays," she admitted. "I guess I just presumed everyone did basically what my family does." "Most people probably think that a little," said Fatima. "It's not really anyone's fault. You just don't think about it until you actually meet someone different." "Would you want to come to my house sometime and see how we celebrate Christmas?" asked Lucy. "I could show you our ornaments and stuff." "I'd really like that," said Fatima, truly pleased by the invitation. That evening, Fatima told her mother about the whole conversation. "Lucy actually wants to learn about Eid properly now," she said. "And she invited me to see her Christmas stuff too." "That's wonderful," said her mother. "The world gets a little smaller and friendlier every time two people actually explain their lives to each other instead of assuming." The following winter, Fatima visited Lucy's house and helped decorate their Christmas tree, and the winter after that, Lucy came to Fatima's Eid celebration and tried maamoul made by Fatima's own hands for the very first time.
