The Parrot Who Only Knew One Sentence

A little English

Present perfect ("has learned," "have been talking"); past perfect continuous introduced ("had been talking to him for years"); conditional structure with modal ("If we want Teto to say something new, we'd have to..."); comparative adverbs (more patiently, more clearly); extended time sequencing across nearly two weeks.

Teto the parrot had learned exactly one phrase from Grandpa Sami: "Who ate the last mango?" Every single day for a week, Teto repeated it at the worst possible moments — during breakfast, during homework time, even once during a video call with Uncle Farouk. Youssef found it hilarious at first. By Thursday, he found it unbearable. "Teto, please, just this once, say something else," Youssef groaned, covering his ears as the parrot squawked the same question for what felt like the hundredth time. His younger sister, Dalia, giggled from the doorway. "Maybe he only knows one sentence because that's the only one anyone bothers repeating to him." Youssef rolled his eyes. "He's a parrot, Dalia. He just says random things." "Actually," said Dalia, "Grandpa told me parrots repeat whatever they hear the most, over and over, until it becomes automatic. If we want Teto to say something new, we'd have to say something new to him constantly, instead of getting annoyed and ignoring him." Youssef considered this. "So it's kind of our fault he only knows one thing?" "Basically," said Dalia. "Nobody's really been talking to him properly. We just react to him." That gave Youssef an idea. Instead of shouting at Teto to stop, he pulled a chair beside the cage and started speaking to him calmly every afternoon. He said simple, friendly phrases: "Good morning, Teto." "Teto is a clever bird." "Time for seeds." At first, nothing changed. Teto still greeted every visitor with his famous mango question. Youssef almost gave up after four days of repetition with no results. "Maybe he's too old to learn something new," Youssef told Dalia, frustrated. "Grandpa's been talking to him for years before we even got him," said Dalia. "Give it more than four days." Youssef kept trying, a little reluctantly, through the following week. He noticed something interesting: the more patiently and clearly he spoke, the more Teto tilted his head and listened, almost as if he were paying closer attention than before. On the twelfth day, right as Youssef walked into the kitchen for breakfast, Teto ruffled his feathers and said, quite clearly, "Good morning!" Youssef nearly dropped his cereal bowl. "Dalia! DALIA! Come here right now!" Dalia rushed in, and together they watched Teto proudly repeat his new phrase three more times, clearly delighted with the reaction he was getting. "He actually did it," Youssef whispered, grinning from ear to ear. "Patience," said Dalia, smiling smugly. "Told you it wasn't his fault." Over the following weeks, Teto's vocabulary grew slowly but steadily — "clever bird," "time for seeds," and eventually, an entire cheerful greeting whenever someone walked through the door. Grandpa Sami, visiting one Sunday, laughed with delight when Teto greeted him warmly instead of asking about mangoes. "Well now," he said, chuckling, "seems like someone's been putting in real effort with this old bird." Youssef smiled proudly. "Turns out, if you want something to change, you can't just get annoyed by it. You have to patiently teach it something better instead."
