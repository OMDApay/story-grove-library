What Did She Say? — Reported Speech

A little English

Reported speech used systematically as the central grammar teaching point ("she said she was proud," "she said you needed," "she said she would collect"); direct speech contrasted with reported speech for clarity; question tags for gentle confirmation ("That's right, isn't it?"); present perfect for accumulated learning ("you've learned"); future forms in reported context ("she said she would give").

After school, Layla came home excited. "Mom! Mom!" she called, rushing into the kitchen. "Ms. Rania said something really interesting today!" "Oh?" said her mother, looking up from chopping vegetables. "What did she say?" Layla paused, thinking. "She said... wait, let me remember exactly. She said, 'I am proud of how hard you all worked on your projects.'" Her mother smiled. "So you're telling me that Ms. Rania said she was proud of how hard you all worked on your projects?" "Yes!" said Layla. "But wait — I said it differently just now. You said 'she was proud' instead of 'I am proud.'" "That's right," said her mother. "When you tell someone what another person said, you often change the words a little. That's called reported speech." Layla looked confused. "Why do we change it?" "Because you're not quoting her exactly," explained her mother. "You're reporting what she said, from your perspective. So 'I' becomes 'she,' 'am' becomes 'was,' and 'you all' becomes 'you' or 'we,' depending on who you're talking to." Layla thought about this. "So if Ms. Rania said, 'You need to study for the test,' I would tell you...?" "You would tell me that Ms. Rania said you needed to study for the test," her mother confirmed. "Got it," said Layla. "What about if she said, 'I will collect your homework tomorrow'?" "Then you would tell me that Ms. Rania said she would collect your homework the next day," said her mother. Layla nodded, starting to understand. "So the words change, but the meaning stays the same?" "Exactly," said her mother. "Reported speech is like translating what someone said into your own words, while keeping the meaning accurate." That evening, Layla told her father about her day. "Ms. Rania said we had done really well on our projects," she reported. "And she said she would give us our grades back on Monday." Her father smiled. "You're using reported speech perfectly," he said. "You changed the words from what Ms. Rania actually said, but you kept the meaning accurate." Layla felt proud. She had learned something new about how language worked — and she hadn't even realized she was learning grammar.
