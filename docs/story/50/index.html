What Would You Do?

A little English

Second conditional used systematically throughout as the central grammar teaching point ("what would you do if...", "I'd probably...", "if it were my wallet, I'd want..."); modal verbs for possibility and advice (might, could, should); reported student dialogue with varied reasoning styles; reflective closing dialogue generalizing the purpose of hypothetical practice.

"What would you do if you found a lost wallet on the sidewalk?" asked Mr. Hassan during ethics class. "Would you keep the money, or try to return it?" The class erupted into chatter. Some students said they'd keep it. Others said they'd return it. A few weren't sure. "Keep in mind," said Mr. Hassan, "there's no single right answer I'm looking for. I want to hear your reasoning. What would you actually do, and why?" Rami raised his hand. "I'd probably try to return it. If it were my wallet, I'd want someone to do the same for me." "Good reasoning," said Mr. Hassan. "You're imagining yourself in the other person's position. That's called perspective-taking." "What if there was no ID in it?" asked Sara. "Then how would you return it?" "That's a great question," said Mr. Hassan. "What would you do in that situation, Sara?" "I guess I'd ask my parents what they think," said Sara. "Or maybe I'd take it to a police station nearby." "Those are both reasonable options," said Mr. Hassan. "Notice how we're using 'would' and 'could' to talk about situations that haven't actually happened? That's called hypothetical reasoning. We're practicing how to think through problems before we face them." The class continued with more scenarios. "What would you do if you saw someone being bullied at school?" "I'd tell a teacher," said Omar. "Because if I tried to stop it myself, I might get hurt too." "What would you do if your friend asked you to copy your homework?" "I'd say no," said Layla. "But I'd offer to help them understand it instead. That way I'm still being a good friend without doing something dishonest." By the end of class, Mr. Hassan had written a list of principles on the board: think about the other person, ask for help when unsure, look for solutions that help everyone, remember that your choices affect other people. "These aren't rules I made up," he said. "These are patterns that thoughtful people have noticed over a long time. You don't have to memorize them. Just keep them in mind when you face real decisions." That evening, Rami told his father about the class discussion. "We talked about what we would do in different situations," he explained. "Not what we should do. What we would actually do." "And how did that feel?" asked his father. "Kind of weird," admitted Rami. "Because there wasn't one exact right answer. But also kind of... grown-up? Like we were actually practicing for real life instead of just memorizing facts." "That's exactly the point," said his father. "Life doesn't give you multiple-choice questions. It gives you situations, and you have to figure out what to do. Practicing that kind of thinking is genuinely useful." Rami considered this. "Can we do more of these 'what would you do' questions at home sometimes?" "Absolutely," said his father, smiling. "I think you're ready for that."
