A little English

Second conditional introduced explicitly for hypothetical safety scenarios ("if someone knew your school AND your name, they could figure out..."); reported speech with full backshift; modal verbs for possibility and advice (could, should, might); question tags for confirmation ("It's not about not trusting anyone, is it?").

Your new classmate, Sami, had just joined the online gaming group chat, and within minutes, someone asked, "What school do you go to, Sami?" Layla, who'd been in the group for months, watched the message appear and felt a small warning bell go off in her head. "Hey," she typed quickly, before Sami could answer. "Remember, we don't share our school names in here. It's private information." The person who'd asked, username "GamerPro99," replied instantly, "Why not? It's just a question." Layla paused, unsure how to explain without sounding like she was lecturing everyone. Her older brother had gone through this exact conversation with her last year, and she remembered his words clearly. She typed carefully: "Because if someone knows your school AND your name, they could figure out where you are at specific times. That's not safe, even if the person asking seems nice." Sami typed back, "Oh, I didn't know that. Thanks, Layla." But GamerPro99 wasn't satisfied. "You're being paranoid. Nobody's going to actually track you down from a school name." Layla felt her stomach tighten slightly. She could either let it go, or she could say something that might make the whole group uncomfortable. She thought about what her brother had told her: "If something feels unsafe online, speak up even if it feels awkward. The awkward feeling lasts five minutes. The unsafe feeling can last a lot longer." She took a breath and typed: "My brother showed me something once. If you know someone's name, their school, and what time their sports practice ends, you could literally know exactly where to find them on certain afternoons. That's not paranoia. That's just how information adds up." A few seconds of silence followed in the chat. Then the group admin, "TeamCaptain," typed: "Layla's right. We have a rule about this. No school names, no addresses, no photos with uniforms or logos that show where you're from. It's not about not trusting anyone. It's about keeping everyone actually safe." GamerPro99 finally replied, "Fine, whatever. I didn't realize it was that serious." "That's the point," Layla typed. "Most people don't realize until someone explains it. Now you know." Later that evening, Layla's brother noticed her looking at her phone thoughtfully. "Everything okay in your gaming group?" "Someone asked Sami what school he goes to," Layla explained. "I told them not to share it, and GamerPro99 called me paranoid." "What did you say back?" asked her brother. "I explained how names plus schools plus schedules could actually add up to something unsafe," said Layla. "And the admin backed me up." Her brother smiled. "That's exactly right. You didn't just say 'don't do it.' You explained why it matters. That's how people actually learn." "Is it weird that I feel kind of proud of myself?" Layla admitted. "It felt self-conscious in the moment, but now I'm glad I said something." "Not weird at all," said her brother. "That self-conscious feeling means you did something that actually mattered, even if it wasn't easy."
