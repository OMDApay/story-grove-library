The Baby Voice

A little English

Past perfect for the behavior pattern's onset ("hadn't done in years," "had started"); present perfect for ongoing noticed pattern ("you've been asking"); reported internal need expressed through simple honest dialogue; conditional proposal ("what if it's just you and me"); resolution dialogue reframing behavior with insight.

Ever since the new baby came home, six-year-old Amir had started doing things he hadn't done in years — asking to be carried, wanting his bottle back instead of his big-kid cup, even speaking in a strange baby voice he'd never used before. "Amir, you're six," his mother said one evening, exhausted, balancing the crying newborn on one shoulder. "You know how to talk normally. Please use your regular voice." "But baby talk," Amir insisted in his squeaky new voice, refusing to switch back. That night, after finally getting the baby to sleep, Amir's mother sat on the edge of his bed, too tired to be frustrated anymore, just curious. "Can we talk about something? I've noticed you've been asking for baby things a lot since your sister arrived. The bottle, the baby voice, wanting to be carried everywhere. What's going on in your head?" Amir was quiet for a long moment. "Everyone keeps looking at the baby," he finally said, his voice small and real again. "Nobody looks at me anymore." His mother's chest clenched. "Oh, Amir. I didn't realize that's how it felt for you." "Maybe if I'm a baby too," said Amir, "you'll look at me the way you look at her." His mother pulled him into a tight hug. "I understand now. You're not actually forgetting how to be six. You're testing something important — whether being smaller and needing more help will get you more attention. That makes complete sense, even though it's confusing for both of us." "So you're not mad?" asked Amir. "I'm not mad at all," said his mother. "But I do want you to know something true. I don't love you any less because your sister is here. Actually, watching you become a big brother is something completely new that I get to love about you now, something your sister doesn't have yet." "Like what?" asked Amir, curious despite himself. "Like how you already know how to talk, walk, tell jokes, and read your favorite book almost by yourself," said his mother. "Your sister can't do any of that yet. She needs the bottle and the baby voice because she genuinely can't do anything else. You get to do so much more, and I love seeing all of it." Amir considered this carefully. "I guess being six does let me do more stuff." "So much more," said his mother. "Tomorrow, once the baby's asleep, what if it's just you and me for twenty minutes? No baby, no bottle, just big-kid time, doing something only six-year-olds get to do." "Can we build the race car track?" asked Amir, his real voice returning fully. "Absolutely," said his mother, smiling with relief. The next afternoon, during their twenty minutes of uninterrupted big-kid time, Amir built an elaborate race track across the living room floor, chattering happily in his completely normal voice the entire time. That evening, when his father asked how the day had gone, Amir declared proudly, "I'm the big brother. I get race tracks. The baby just gets bottles." His mother caught her husband's eye and smiled. "Turns out, he didn't actually want to be a baby again. He just wanted to know he still mattered just as much as one."
