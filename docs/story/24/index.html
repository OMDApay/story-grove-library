The Cardboard City

"I'm bored," Huda announced for the fourth time in ten minutes, sprawled dramatically across the living room couch.

A little English

Past continuous for background state ("was reading"); past habitual "used to" reinforced from Batch 3; present perfect for unnoticed duration ("had built," "two hours passed without noticing"); reported childhood memory nested in dialogue; conditional reflection in closing ("never would have thought about... if").

"I'm bored," Huda announced for the fourth time in ten minutes, sprawled dramatically across the living room couch. "Can I please have the tablet? Just for a little while?" "The tablet's charging in my room, and it's staying there today," said her father, not looking up from the book he was reading. "You'll have to find something else." "But there's nothing else to do," groaned Huda. "Everything in this house is boring." Her father finally looked up. "You know, when I was your age, we didn't have tablets at all. My brother and I used to say we were bored constantly. My mother had a rule: if you tell me you're bored, I'll find you a chore to do instead." "That seems mean," said Huda. "It worked, though," said her father, smiling at the memory. "After enough chores, we got very creative about finding our own fun instead of complaining about being bored." Huda flopped back onto the couch dramatically, but after a few more silent minutes, curiosity got the better of her. She wandered into the garage, poking through a box of old materials her mother kept for craft projects — cardboard tubes, bottle caps, mismatched buttons, and a tangle of colorful yarn. At first, she just moved things around without any real plan. But slowly, an idea started forming. She began stacking cardboard tubes together, taping them at odd angles, adding bottle caps as decorations. Two hours passed without Huda noticing. By the time her father checked on her, she had built an intricate cardboard city on the garage floor, complete with towers, a "river" made of blue yarn, and tiny bottle-cap cars parked along cardboard streets. "Huda, this is incredible," said her father, crouching down to examine the tiny details. "You built all of this from junk in a box?" "I didn't even notice the time passing," said Huda, surprised herself. "I kept thinking of one more thing to add." That evening, Huda's younger brother wandered in and immediately wanted to add his own building to the cardboard city. Together, they expanded it across half the garage floor, arguing happily about where the river should curve and whether dinosaurs were allowed to visit the cardboard town. At dinner, Huda's mother asked how the afternoon had gone. "I was SO bored earlier," said Huda, "but then I built an entire city. It's honestly better than anything on the tablet." "Boredom is strange that way," said her father. "It feels terrible for the first ten minutes. But if you actually sit with it instead of immediately filling it up with a screen, your brain eventually starts inventing things on its own. That's usually where the best ideas come from." "Can we keep the cardboard city up for a while?" asked Huda. "I want to add a bridge tomorrow." "As long as it stays in the garage," laughed her mother, "you can keep building it for as long as you want." That night, lying in bed, Huda found herself already planning the bridge design in her head, something she never would have thought about if the tablet had simply been handed to her the moment she complained.
