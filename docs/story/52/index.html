The New Baby

When baby Omar was born, everything in Sami's house seemed to revolve around him.

A little English

Second conditional for hypothetical reflection ("what if we made some changes?"); passive voice ("it was divided"); reported dialogue with emotional validation; question tags for gentle confirmation ("It hasn't been easy, has it?"); present perfect for accumulated feeling ("I've been making you feel").

When baby Omar was born, everything in Sami's house seemed to revolve around him. "Shh, the baby is sleeping," his mother would whisper whenever Sami wanted to play loudly."Can you bring me a diaper?" his father would ask, even when Sami was in the middle of building something."Look how cute he is!" every visitor would say, barely glancing at Sami before cooing over the baby. One evening, after a particularly difficult day where Sami had been asked to be quiet five separate times, he crashed. "I hate the baby!" he shouted, slamming his bedroom door. "Nobody cares about me anymore!" His mother knocked gently on the door. "Can I come in?" "No," said Sami, but she opened it anyway and sat down on the edge of his bed. "I heard what you said," she said quietly. "And I want you to know something: it's okay to feel that way sometimes. Having a new baby in the house is really hard for big brothers." Sami stared at his blanket, not meeting her eyes. "You probably love him more than me now." His mother's expression softened. "Oh, Sami. That's not how love works. Love isn't like a cake that gets smaller when you share it. When Omar was born, my love for you didn't get divided. It just... grew. To make room for him too." "But it feels divided," Sami admitted, his voice barely above a whisper. "Like I'm always the one who has to be quiet, to help, to wait." "You're right," said his mother. "It does feel that way sometimes. And I'm sorry we've been making you feel like you're less important. You're not. You're just different now — you're the big brother, and that comes with different responsibilities." She paused, thinking. "What if we made some changes? What if you and I had special time together, just the two of us, every single week? No baby, no interruptions. Just us." Sami looked up, surprised. "Really?" "Really," said his mother. "And you know what else? Omar is going to learn so much from you. He's going to watch how you build things, how you read, how you solve problems. You're going to be his first teacher, whether you realize it or not." Sami considered this. "I never thought about it that way." "Most big brothers don't," said his mother. "But it's true. And sometimes, when you're feeling jealous or left out, it helps to remember that." The following week, Sami's mother kept her promise. Every Tuesday afternoon, she and Sami went to the park together while his father stayed home with the baby. No phones, no distractions, just the two of them. One Tuesday, as they were walking home, Sami asked, "Do you think Omar will actually remember all the stuff I teach him?" "Maybe not consciously," said his mother. "But he'll learn from watching you. That's how little kids learn — by copying the people they love." Sami smiled slightly. "I guess that's kind of cool." "It is," said his mother. "And you know what else? I'm really proud of how you've been handling all these changes. It hasn't been easy, but you've been patient and kind, even when you didn't feel like it." That evening, when Sami went to say goodnight to the baby, he found Omar awake in his crib, staring up at him with wide eyes. "Hey, little guy," Sami whispered. "Tomorrow I'm going to show you how to build a tower. You're going to love it." Omar cooed softly, as if he understood. From his doorway, Sami's mother watched the whole exchange silently, her heart full. The jealousy hadn't disappeared completely — she knew it would come and go — but for this moment, at least, her big boy felt seen, valued, and loved exactly as much as he always had been.
