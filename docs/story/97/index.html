The Listening Game

A little English

Reported speech for teacher instruction ("Ms. Dina explained that active listening has four parts"); contrast clauses for conceptual distinction ("not just hearing words — it's about showing you care"); question tags for gentle confirmation ("That's right, isn't it?"); present perfect for accumulated realization ("you've learned"); future forms for prediction ("you'll stand out").

During circle time, Ms. Dina introduced a new game. "Today," she said, "we're practicing active listening. That means listening with your whole body — not just your ears." The class looked curious. "Active listening has four parts," explained Ms. Dina. "First: eye contact. Look at the person who's speaking. Second: still body. Sit or stand still, facing the speaker. Third: clarifying questions. Ask questions to make sure you understand. And fourth: the pause rule. Wait two to three seconds after someone finishes before you respond." The class practiced with a partner. Sara spoke for one minute about her weekend while Youssef listened actively. "I went to the park with my family," said Sara. "We had a picnic and played frisbee." When Sara finished, Youssef waited three seconds. Then he asked, "Who else was at the park?" "Great clarifying question," said Ms. Dina, watching nearby. Sara answered. "My aunt and uncle came too. And my cousin." Youssef nodded. "So it was a big family gathering?" "Exactly," said Sara, smiling. "You understood." "That's active listening," said Ms. Dina. "You showed Sara you were paying attention — with your eyes, your body, and your questions." That afternoon, Youssef told his mother about the game. "I used to think listening was just hearing words," he explained. "But now I know it's about showing you care and understand." "That's exactly right," said his mother. "Active listening is a skill that helps people feel heard and valued. And you know what? Most people don't listen well — so if you master this skill, you'll stand out." Youssef nodded. He had learned something important: listening isn't passive — it's active. And showing someone you're listening makes them feel valued and understood.
