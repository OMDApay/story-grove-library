The Card Tournament Under the Tree

A new student joined Rana's class in the middle of the school year, a boy named Adam who used a wheelchair to get around.

A little English

Past continuous for scene-setting ("was spinning," "were playing"); reported explanation dialogue; comparative reasoning ("just as much, maybe even more"); present perfect for accumulated change ("four more classmates had joined"); reflective closing dialogue about decision-making under uncertainty.

A new student joined Rana's class in the middle of the school year, a boy named Adam who used a wheelchair to get around. During the first morning, a few kids whispered and stared as Adam wheeled himself to a desk near the front. At recess, most of the class ran outside to the playground equipment, while Adam stayed near the classroom entrance, watching from a distance since the climbing structure had steps he couldn't use. Rana noticed him sitting there, alone, spinning one wheel slowly with his hand. "Why isn't he playing with everyone?" Rana asked her older cousin Nour, who worked as a teaching assistant at the school. "The playground structure only has stairs," said Nour. "Adam can't climb stairs in his wheelchair, so there's nothing built for him to actually join in with, even if he wanted to." "That doesn't seem fair," said Rana. "Everyone else gets a way to play, but he doesn't?" "You're right," said Nour. "The school is actually planning to build a ramp next year. But that doesn't help him today, does it?" Rana thought about this during the rest of recess, glancing occasionally at Adam sitting quietly by himself. The next day, instead of running straight to the climbing structure, Rana walked over to Adam instead. "Hi, I'm Rana. Do you want to play something that doesn't need the climbing thing?" Adam looked up, a little surprised. "Like what?" "I don't know," Rana admitted. "What do you usually play at your old school?" "Wheelchair basketball, mostly," said Adam. "And this card game called Speed. I'm really fast at it." "We don't have a basketball hoop here," said Rana, "but I definitely have cards in my backpack." They played three rounds of Speed under a shaded tree near the entrance. Adam won every single round, laughing each time Rana groaned about losing again. By the end of the week, four more classmates had joined the card games during recess, gathering under the same tree, taking turns and creating tournament brackets. Adam, it turned out, was startlingly good at organizing games — quick with rules, fair with turns, endlessly patient with slower players. "You're really good at this," said a boy named Faris one afternoon, after Adam had explained a tricky rule clearly for the third time. "I've had a lot of practice," said Adam. "When you can't always join the running games, you get really good at the sitting-down ones instead." One month later, when the wheelchair ramp construction finally began near the old climbing structure, Adam mentioned it casually during recess. "Are you excited?" asked Rana. "You'll finally be able to use the climbing thing." Adam considered this. "Kind of. But honestly, I like our card tournament group just as much. Maybe even more." Rana smiled. "We'll keep doing both, then. New ramp, plus card tournaments. Best of everything." That evening, Rana told her mother about the ramp, the cards, and the tournament bracket taped to the classroom wall. "I almost didn't say anything to him that first day," she admitted. "I didn't know what to say, so I almost said nothing at all." "What made you finally go talk to him?" asked her mother. "I kept thinking about how lonely he looked," said Rana. "And I figured lonely was worse than saying something a little awkward."
