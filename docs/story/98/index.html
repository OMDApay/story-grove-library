The Mediation Circle

When Layla and her friend, Nour, had a disagreement, they didn't know how to fix it.

A little English

Reported speech for multi-turn mediation ("Ms. Rania said that step one is to name the problem"); contrast clauses for conceptual distinction ("not about winning — it's about finding a solution that works for both"); question tags for gentle confirmation ("That's right, isn't it?"); present perfect for accumulated realization ("you've learned"); future forms for follow-up ("we'll see how it's going").

When Layla and her friend, Nour, had a disagreement, they didn't know how to fix it. "She took my pencil without asking," said Layla, upset. "But I needed it!" said Nour. "And I was going to give it back!" Ms. Rania, who had been watching, pulled them aside. "You two have a conflict," she said gently. "Let's resolve it using the five-step model." The girls looked curious. "Step one," said Ms. Rania, "is to name the problem. What happened?" "Layla's pencil was taken without asking," said Nour. "And I felt upset because it was my favorite pencil," added Layla. "Step two," continued Ms. Rania, "is to identify what each person needs. What do you need, Layla?" "I need people to ask before borrowing my things," said Layla. "And what do you need, Nour?" "I need to be able to borrow things when I'm in a hurry," said Nour. "But I also need Layla to know I'll return them." "Step three," said Ms. Rania, "is to brainstorm solutions. What are some ideas?" The girls thought. "Nour could ask first," suggested Layla. "Or I could keep an extra pencil in my bag to lend," offered Nour. "Or we could have a 'borrowing box' with spare pencils," added Layla. "Great ideas," said Ms. Rania. "Step four is to choose one solution and try it. Which one do you want to try?" "Let's try the asking-first rule," said Nour. "And I'll keep an extra pencil in my bag just in case." "Perfect," said Ms. Rania. "Step five is to check back and adjust if needed. In a few days, we'll see how it's going." That evening, Layla told her mother about the mediation. "I used to think conflicts just ended when people stopped fighting," she explained. "But now I know there's a process to actually resolve them." "That's exactly right," said her mother. "Conflict resolution isn't about winning — it's about finding a solution that works for both people. And that's a skill you'll use your whole life." Layla nodded. She had learned something important: conflicts don't have to end friendships — they can strengthen them, if you resolve them with respect and understanding.
