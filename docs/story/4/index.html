The Race for the Last Seat

Rain hammered against the school bus windows as it screeched to a stop outside Riverside Elementary.

A little English

Simple past tense; past continuous for ongoing background action; comparative feelings expressions (harder this time, odd twist); dialogue involving negotiation and mutual agreement.

Rain hammered against the school bus windows as it screeched to a stop outside Riverside Elementary. Twenty-six children scrambled up the wet steps, shaking off umbrellas and dripping raincoats. Only one empty seat remained — the good one, next to the window, away from the wobbly back wheel that made the whole bus bounce. Hassan spotted it first. He sprinted down the narrow aisle, backpack bouncing, and dove into the seat just as another boy, Malik, reached for it too. "Hey! I was going for that one!" said Malik, standing awkwardly in the aisle as the bus lurched forward. "Too slow," said Hassan, smirking, and turned to look out the rain-streaked window. Malik shuffled to the back, dropping heavily into the bumpy seat above the wobbly wheel. Every pothole sent him bouncing painfully into the seat's hard metal frame. Hassan glanced back once, saw Malik wincing with each bump, and felt an odd twist in his stomach. He tried to ignore it and stared harder out the window. Halfway through the ride, the bus hit an enormous pothole. Malik yelped as his head nearly hit the ceiling. A few kids laughed. Malik's ears turned red, and he stared down at his shoes, saying nothing. Hassan's stomach twisted again, harder this time. He thought about how he would feel if everyone laughed at him like that. At the next stop, an idea formed in Hassan's mind. When the bus began moving again, Hassan called out, "Malik, want to trade seats for the rest of the ride?" Malik looked up, surprised. "Wait — really? You got there first." "I know," said Hassan. "But I got the good seat every day this week. It's your turn." Malik moved carefully down the swaying aisle and slid into the window seat with a relieved sigh. "Thanks, Hassan. That wheel back there is brutal." "I noticed," said Hassan, settling into the bumpy back seat. It jolted painfully on the very next pothole, and Hassan understood exactly what Malik had been feeling. To his surprise, the bumpy ride didn't feel so bad once he'd chosen it himself. There was something satisfying about the trade that made even the bouncing seem less annoying. When the bus finally reached Hassan's stop, Malik called after him, "Same time tomorrow? We could just switch every other day." Hassan grinned. "Deal." That evening, Hassan told his older sister about the bus and the trade. "I didn't have to give up my seat," he said. "But watching Malik get bounced around all week just felt wrong, once I actually paid attention." His sister nodded. "That's called empathy — noticing how someone else feels, even when it's not happening to you. It's easy to ignore. It's harder, but better, to act on it." The next morning, Hassan and Malik boarded the bus together, laughing about something from class. Neither one raced for the window seat. They had already agreed whose turn it was.
