The Empty Balcony

Every single day after school, Bilal walked past the same elderly neighbor, Umm Khaled, sitting alone on her balcony, and every single day, he simply waved and kept walking.

A little English

Present perfect for ongoing habitual action ("had become such a habit," "hasn't been able"); past perfect for backstory ("had waved," "had never once asked," "had taught"); relative clauses ("who visited only twice a year," "which she used to tend"); extended time markers (a month later, three more times that week).

Every single day after school, Bilal walked past the same elderly neighbor, Umm Khaled, sitting alone on her balcony, and every single day, he simply waved and kept walking. It had become such a habit that he barely noticed her anymore, the way you stop noticing a picture that's hung on the same wall for years. One rainy Tuesday, Bilal's football practice ended early, and instead of rushing home as usual, he found himself walking slower than normal. As he passed Umm Khaled's building, he noticed something different — her balcony was empty for the first time in longer than he could remember. Bilal paused at the gate, an odd, uneasy feeling settling in his chest. He wasn't sure why, exactly, but something about the empty balcony bothered him. He knocked on the building's front door. An elderly man he didn't recognize, perhaps a neighbor, answered. "I'm looking for Umm Khaled," said Bilal. "She usually sits on her balcony. Is everything alright?" "She's had a bad cough for three days," said the man. "Hasn't been able to come outside. I think she lives alone, poor thing. Her children live in another city." Bilal felt something twist uncomfortably in his stomach. Umm Khaled had waved at him nearly every single day for over a year, and he had never once asked her name, or knocked on her door, or wondered whether she had anyone checking on her. That evening, Bilal told his mother everything. "I didn't even know her actual name until today," he admitted. "I just called her Umm Khaled because that's what everyone calls her." His mother thought for a moment. "Would you like to bring her some soup tomorrow? I'm making a big pot anyway." The next afternoon, Bilal knocked nervously on Umm Khaled's door, holding a warm container of soup. When she opened it, looking tired but pleasantly surprised, Bilal suddenly felt shy in a way he hadn't expected. "I brought you some soup," he said. "My mom made too much, and... well, I noticed you weren't on your balcony." Umm Khaled's eyes softened. "You noticed?" "I guess I did," said Bilal, a little embarrassed. "I just never really stopped to talk before. I'm sorry about that." "You're here now," she said warmly, inviting him to sit for a few minutes. She told him stories about her grandchildren, who visited only twice a year, and about her garden, which she used to tend before her knees made it difficult. Bilal visited three more times that week, sometimes just for ten minutes, sometimes staying longer to hear another story. A month later, Bilal's short visits had become a regular part of his afternoon, right after football practice. Umm Khaled had taught him how to fold paper boats, the same way she'd once taught her own children decades earlier. "You know," Bilal told his mother one evening, "I used to walk past her every single day without really seeing her. Now I think she might be one of my favorite parts of the whole week." His mother smiled. "That's what happens when you actually stop and look at the people around you, instead of just walking past them."
