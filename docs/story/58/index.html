Looking After Luna

When Sara's family got a new puppy, her parents said, "You'll need to look after it properly if you want to keep it." "I will," Sara promised.

A little English

Phrasal verbs used systematically throughout as the central grammar teaching point ("look after," "give up," "figure out," "get along," "clean up after"); second conditional for hypothetical reflection ("if you could go back"); reported advice dialogue with practical life-skills framing; question tags for gentle confirmation ("That's frustrating, isn't it?").

When Sara's family got a new puppy, her parents said, "You'll need to look after it properly if you want to keep it." "I will," Sara promised. "I'll feed it, walk it, and clean up after it. I won't give up even when it's hard." The first week went smoothly. The puppy, whom they named Luna, was playful and affectionate, following Sara everywhere she went. But by the second week, Sara started to figure out that having a puppy was more work than she'd envisioned. "Luna chewed up my homework again," Sara complained one evening, holding up a shredded math worksheet. Her father looked at the mess sympathetically. "That's frustrating. But remember, she's still learning what she can and can't chew. It's up to us to teach her." "But I'm tired of picking up after her," Sara admitted. "I thought it would be easier." "It is a lot of work," agreed her father. "That's why we said you'd need to look after her properly. Are you thinking about giving up?" Sara hesitated. "A little. But then I think about how sad Luna would be if we gave her away. And I don't want to let her down." "That's really sensible of you," said her father. "Sometimes looking after something means doing the hard parts, not just the fun parts." The following week, Sara's teacher assigned a group project, and Sara was paired with Ahmed, a boy she didn't get along with very well. "I don't want to work with him," Sara told her mother. "We never agree on anything." "Sometimes we have to figure out how to work with people we don't organically click with," said her mother. "That's a life skill, not just a school skill." Sara sighed. "I guess. But what if we can't figure it out?" "Then you'll ask for help," said her mother. "But I bet if you both try to meet halfway, you'll get along better than you think." Sara took a deep breath and texted Ahmed to set up a meeting. To her surprise, he was friendly and willing to compromise, and by the end of their first work session, they had figured out a plan that used both of their strengths. "I didn't think we'd get along this well," Sara admitted to her mother that evening. "See?" said her mother. "Sometimes you have to put in the effort to figure things out before they start working." By the end of the month, Sara had established a routine with Luna: morning walks before school, afternoon playtime, evening feeding and cleanup. It wasn't always easy — Luna still chewed things occasionally, and Sara still had to pick up surprises from the floor — but she hadn't given up. "You've been looking after Luna really well," her father noted one evening, watching Sara patiently teach Luna to sit. "I have," said Sara proudly. "And you know what? I actually don't want to give this up anymore. It's hard, but it's also kind of rewarding." "That's exactly how responsibility works," said her father. "It's not always fun, but it's always worth it." That weekend, Sara's cousin visited and watched the whole routine in action. "How do you remember to do all this every single day?" she asked, amazed. Sara thought about it. "At first, I had to remind myself constantly. But now it's just... what I do. Like brushing my teeth. I don't think about it anymore — I just do it." "That's what habits are," said her father, overhearing. "Things you do automatically because you've done them enough times. And that's how you know you've really figured something out — when it becomes part of your routine, not just something you try occasionally." Sara smiled, watching Luna trot happily beside her as they headed out for their evening walk. She had looked after something difficult, and she hadn't given up. That felt better than any easy success ever could have.
