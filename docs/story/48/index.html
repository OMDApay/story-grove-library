The Boredom List

"Nour, you've been on that tablet for almost two hours," her mother called from the kitchen.

A little English

Second conditional for hypothetical planning ("what if we made a list"); modal verbs for suggestion and possibility (could, can, might); reported teacher dialogue with indirect speech; present perfect for accumulated change ("has been drawing more"); question tags for gentle confirmation ("They're just one option, aren't they?").

"Nour, you've been on that tablet for almost two hours," her mother called from the kitchen. "Time to take a break." "But I'm in the middle of something!" Nour protested, not looking up from her building game. "I can't just pause it." "You absolutely can," said her mother, walking over and gently setting a hand on her shoulder. "The game will still be there in twenty minutes. Your eyes and your brain need a break." Nour sighed theatrically but set the tablet down. "Now what am I supposed to do? I'm bored already." "That's actually a good sign," said her mother. "It means your brain is ready for something different. What if we made a list of things you could do instead of screens when you're done for the day?" "Like what?" asked Nour, skeptical. "Let's think together," said her mother. "What do you enjoy that doesn't involve a screen at all?" Nour considered this. "I like drawing. And sometimes I build things with my blocks. And I guess I like helping you cook, even though I always say I don't." Her mother smiled. "Those are all great options. What if we made a 'boredom list' and posted it on the fridge? Then when you're done with screen time and feel bored, you can pick something from the list instead of asking me what to do." They spent the next ten minutes writing and decorating a colorful list: draw a picture, build something with blocks, help make dinner, read a book, play outside, call a friend, organize your toys. "Can I add 'make a fort with blankets'?" asked Nour. "Absolutely," said her mother, writing it down. That evening, after dinner, Nour's screen time was finished for the day. She glanced at the tablet, then at the new list on the fridge. "I'm going to make a fort," she announced, dragging cushions and blankets into the living room. Her father, watching from the couch, smiled. "That looks impressive. What's the plan?" "It's going to be a reading fort," Nour explained. "I'm going to read my new book inside it." Her mother caught her father's eye from the kitchen doorway, both of them quietly pleased. A week later, Nour's teacher mentioned during parent-teacher conferences that Nour had been drawing more during free time at school instead of asking to use the classroom tablet. "She told me she's been practicing at home," the teacher said. "She said she has a 'boredom list' now." Nour's mother smiled. "We do. It's made a surprisingly big difference." That evening, Nour told her mother about the teacher's comment. "I showed Sara my drawing and she wanted to make her own list too," she said. "How does it feel to have a plan instead of just being told 'no more screens'?" asked her mother. "Better," said Nour truthfully. "It doesn't feel like I'm losing something. It feels like I'm choosing something else instead." "That's exactly the point," said her mother. "Screens aren't bad. They're just one option among many. Your list reminds you that you have lots of other options too."
