The Sandwich Thief

A little English

Past perfect continuous for the ongoing pattern ("had been blocking," "had been doing"); reported threats and reported speech; present perfect for recent repeated events ("you've asked... four times"); extended multi-week time sequencing; direct dialogue modeling appropriate adult response.

Every single recess for the past three weeks, a bigger boy named Fahd had been blocking Nasser's path to the swings, shoving his shoulder just hard enough to make him stumble, and taking his snack from his backpack without asking. "That's my sandwich," Nasser said quietly on the fourth Monday in a row, watching Fahd unwrap it anyway. "Finders keepers," said Fahd, laughing with two boys standing beside him. "Cry about it." Nasser didn't cry, not at school anyway. But every morning, his stomach twisted into knots just thinking about recess, and he had started making excuses to stay inside during break time, pretending to feel sick. His teacher, Miss Reem, eventually noticed the pattern. "Nasser, you've asked to stay inside during recess four times this week. Is everything okay?" Nasser hesitated. Fahd had warned him once, quietly, that telling a teacher would make things "so much worse." "I'm fine," Nasser mumbled, looking at his shoes. Miss Reem knelt down gently. "Nasser, I've noticed you flinch a little whenever Fahd walks near you at lineup. That's not nothing. You can tell me, and I promise it will help, not make things harder." Nasser's throat tightened. Slowly, he told her everything — the shoving, the stolen snacks, the threats to stay quiet, all of it, weeks of it, spilling out at once. Miss Reem listened without interrupting, her expression serious but calm. "Thank you for trusting me with this, Nasser. This is exactly the kind of thing you should always tell a trusted adult about, even if someone tells you not to. What Fahd has been doing is bullying, and it is never okay, no matter what he says about it getting worse." "Will he get in big trouble?" asked Nasser nervously. "He'll face consequences, and he'll get help understanding why this behavior isn't acceptable," said Miss Reem. "But more importantly, you'll be safe at recess again. That matters most." That afternoon, Miss Reem spoke privately with Fahd, and separately, with the school principal and Fahd's parents. Nasser didn't witness the conversations, but the very next day, Fahd was moved to a different recess group, supervised more closely by a different teacher. For the first week, Nasser still felt nervous walking to the swings, glancing over his shoulder out of habit. But Fahd never approached him again. Two weeks later, during a school assembly about kindness, Miss Reem spoke to the whole school about the difference between joking around and bullying, using no names, but describing exactly the kind of pattern Nasser had experienced — repeated, unwanted, and making someone afraid to come to school. After the assembly, a girl named Huda, from a different class, approached Nasser quietly. "Something like that happened to me last year," she said. "I never told anyone. I wish I had told someone sooner, like you did." Nasser felt something warm replace the nervous knot he'd carried for weeks. "It felt scary to tell at first," he admitted. "But it actually did get better. Fast, too." That evening, Nasser told his parents the whole story for the first time, including the part where he'd been too afraid to say anything for three whole weeks. "I'm proud of you for telling Miss Reem when you finally did," said his father. "And I want you to remember something for the rest of your life: telling a trusted adult about bullying isn't tattling. It's asking for help with something that isn't your fault, and it's always, always the right thing to do."
