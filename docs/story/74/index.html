The Homework Choice — Delayed Consequences

Adam had a choice to make. He could finish his homework now, before dinner, and have the whole evening free. Or he could play video games now and do his homework later…

A little English

Future forms for consequence prediction ("I'll be free," "I'll have to do"); reported speech for teacher's advice ("his teacher had said that choices affect tomorrow"); contrast clauses for comparing options ("whether immediate or delayed"); question tags for reflective discussion ("That's better, isn't it?"); present perfect for accumulated understanding ("you've finished").

Adam had a choice to make. He could finish his homework now, before dinner, and have the whole evening free. Or he could play video games now and do his homework later, after dinner. "If I do it now," he told himself, "I'll be free all evening. But if I play now, I'll have to do it when I'm tired." He thought about what his teacher had said that week: "Your choices today affect your tomorrow. Sometimes the effects are immediate. Sometimes they're delayed. But they're always there." Adam chose to play video games. At 7 PM, after dinner, he sat down to do his homework. But he was already tired. His eyes felt heavy, and his brain felt foggy. "This is harder than it would've been this afternoon," he admitted to his mother. "That's often how delayed consequences work," said his mother. "The consequence of choosing to play first isn't immediate. It shows up later, when you're trying to do something hard while tired." Adam sighed and kept working. By the time he finished, it was 9 PM — an hour later than his bedtime. "Tomorrow," he told his mother, "I'm going to do my homework first, before I play." "How do you think that will feel?" asked his mother. "Better," said Adam. "Because I'll be done, and I can play without worrying about homework hanging over me." "That's exactly right," said his mother. "Delayed consequences are tricky because they don't hit you right away. But if you think ahead — if you ask yourself, 'How will I feel about this choice in two hours?' — you can make better decisions." The next day, Adam did his homework right after school. He finished by 5 PM, and the rest of the evening felt light and free. "This feels so much better," he told his mother. "That's the reward of thinking ahead," she said. "The consequence of doing homework first isn't immediate either. But it shows up later, as free time and peace of mind." Adam nodded. He was starting to understand: every choice had consequences, whether immediate or delayed. The trick was learning to see them before they hit.
