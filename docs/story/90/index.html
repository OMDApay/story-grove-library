The Reason Why — Complex Sentences

Layla was trying to explain why she was upset.

A little English

Complex sentences used systematically as the central grammar teaching point ("I'm sad because my friend moved away," "although I know we can still video call," "I'm happy since I finished," "I'll play while the sun is shining," "unless you need help"); reported speech for grammar explanation ("her mother explained that subordinate clauses can't stand alone"); contrast clauses for conceptual distinction ("it needs the main clause to make sense"); question tags for gentle confirmation ("That's right, isn't it?"); present perfect for accumulated realization ("you've realized").

Layla was trying to explain why she was upset. "I'm sad," she told her mother, "because my friend moved away. And although I know we can still video call, it's not the same as seeing her every day." Her mother listened carefully. "You're using complex sentences," she observed. "You said 'because my friend moved away' — that's a subordinate clause explaining why you're sad. And you said 'although I know we can still video call' — that's another subordinate clause showing contrast." Layla looked confused. "What's a subordinate clause?" "A subordinate clause is a part of a sentence that can't stand alone," explained her mother. "It needs the main clause to make sense. Like 'because my friend moved away' — that doesn't make sense by itself. But 'I'm sad because my friend moved away' does." "Got it," said Layla. "So I could say, 'I'm happy since I finished my homework'? Or 'I'll play outside while the sun is shining'?" "Exactly," said her mother. "You're using subordinating conjunctions — words like 'because,' 'although,' 'since,' 'while,' and 'unless' — to connect clauses and add nuance to your thinking." "That's a lot of words," said Layla. "It is," agreed her mother. "But you're already using them correctly in your speech. You just didn't know the grammar names for them." That evening, Layla told her father about her day. "I'm tired because I had a long day at school," she reported. "And although I want to play, I know I should rest." Her father smiled. "You're using complex sentences perfectly," he said. "'Because I had a long day' — that explains why you're tired. 'Although I want to play' — that shows contrast between what you want and what you should do." Layla felt proud. She hadn't realized she was using complex grammar structures — but now that she knew, she noticed them everywhere. "I'll read my book unless you need help with something," she told her father. "And since it's almost bedtime, I should get ready." Her father laughed. "That's perfect," he said. "'Unless you need help' — that's a condition. 'Since it's almost bedtime' — that's a reason. You've got it."
