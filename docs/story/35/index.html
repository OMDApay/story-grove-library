The Shoes That Hurt

For the fourth morning in a row, Sami refused to put on his shoes, collapsing onto the hallway floor in tears while his mother checked the clock anxiously.

A little English

Past continuous for recurring morning scene; conditional collaborative proposal ("what if we tried"); reported honest explanation dialogue; present perfect for accumulated wrong assumption ("I spent weeks assuming"); resolution dialogue reframing "difficult behavior" as "unaddressed problem."

For the fourth morning in a row, Sami refused to put on his shoes, collapsing onto the hallway floor in tears while his mother checked the clock anxiously. "We're going to be late again," she said, frustration creeping into her voice. "Just put on your shoes, Sami. We do this every single morning." "NO!" Sami shouted, kicking the shoes away from himself. That evening, exhausted from another difficult morning, Sami's mother called her sister, a school counselor, for advice. "Instead of trying to fix the shoe problem directly," her sister suggested, "try asking him what's actually getting in the way, when both of you are calm, not during the rush." That weekend, with no rush and no deadline, Sami's mother sat down on the floor beside him. "Can I ask you something? Not about being late. I'm just curious what happens for you with the shoes in the morning." Sami hesitated, unused to being asked instead of told. "They feel weird," he finally admitted. "The seam inside rubs against my toes and it hurts, but only in the morning when my feet feel puffy." His mother blinked, genuinely surprised. "I had no idea they actually hurt. I thought you just didn't want to stop playing." "Sometimes that too," Sami admitted honestly. "But mostly it's the seam thing." "Why didn't you tell me that before?" asked his mother gently, not accusingly. "I tried once," said Sami. "But you were rushing, and it came out sounding like an excuse, so I just stopped trying to explain." His mother felt a small pang of guilt. "That makes so much sense now. Let's actually solve this together. What if we tried different socks, thicker ones, to protect that spot?" They experimented that weekend with three different sock types. The thickest pair solved the problem almost completely, cushioning the seam so it no longer rubbed uncomfortably. The following Monday, Sami put on his shoes and thick new socks without a single complaint, ready five minutes earlier than usual. "How do they feel?" asked his mother, almost afraid to hope. "Actually fine," said Sami, sounding surprised himself. "Really fine." That evening, Sami's mother told her sister how the conversation had gone. "I spent weeks assuming he was just being difficult on purpose," she admitted. "It turns out there was an actual reason the whole time. I just never asked calmly enough to find out what it was." "That's usually how it works," said her sister. "Kids aren't always great at explaining problems clearly, especially during a stressful rush. But if you ask what's actually getting in their way, during a calm moment, you often find a real, solvable reason underneath what looks like stubbornness." "I feel a little bad I didn't ask sooner," said Sami's mother. "Don't be too hard on yourself," said her sister. "Now you know for next time. That's what actually matters." The next morning, watching Sami tie his shoes independently and cheerfully, his mother felt something shift in how she saw him — not as a child being difficult, but as a child who had been quietly struggling with something small that nobody had thought to ask about.
