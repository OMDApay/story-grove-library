Walking in Her Shoes

When Youssef's sister, Amina, got upset about something small, he didn't understand.

A little English

Passive voice for emotional states ("wasn't sure," "had been watching"); reported speech for mother's teaching ("his mother said that perspective-taking is stepping into someone else's shoes"); contrast clauses for nuanced understanding ("even if it's different from what you expected"); question tags for gentle confirmation ("That's right, isn't it?"); present perfect for accumulated realization ("you've learned").

When Youssef's sister, Amina, got upset about something small, he didn't understand. "It's just a pencil," he said. "Why are you so upset?" Amina wiped her eyes. "It's not just a pencil. It's the pencil that Grandma gave me before she passed away." Youssef felt a lump in his throat. He hadn't known that. "I'm sorry," he said. "I didn't realize it was special." His mother, who had been watching from the kitchen, pulled him aside. "Youssef," she said gently, "sometimes when people get upset about something that seems small to you, it's because that thing means something different to them." "Like the pencil?" asked Youssef. "Exactly," said his mother. "To you, it was just a pencil. But to Amina, it was a memory of Grandma. That's why she was so upset." Youssef thought about this. "So I should try to see things from her perspective?" "Precisely," said his mother. "That's called perspective-taking — stepping into someone else's shoes and seeing the situation from their point of view." "How do I do that?" asked Youssef. "Ask yourself, 'What might this person be feeling? What might this situation mean to them?' Then listen to their answer, even if it's different from what you expected." That evening, Youssef went to Amina's room. "I'm sorry I broke your pencil," he said. "I didn't know it was from Grandma. But now I understand why it was so special to you." Amina looked up, surprised. "Thank you for understanding," she said. "I know it seems like a small thing. But it was one of the last things she gave me." Youssef sat down beside her. "Can I help you find another special pencil? Maybe we could go to the store together and pick one out?" Amina smiled through her tears. "That would be nice," she said. "But can we also keep the broken pieces? I want to remember the pencil that Grandma gave me." "Of course," said Youssef. "We can keep it in a special box, as a memory." Amina hugged her brother. "Thank you for trying to understand," she said. That night, Youssef told his mother what had happened. "I tried to see things from Amina's perspective," he explained. "And it helped me understand why she was so upset." His mother smiled. "That's empathy," she said. "And it's one of the most important skills you can learn."
