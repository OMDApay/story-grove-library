The Study Schedule

A little English

Direct comparison of two praise phrasings as the central grammar and rhetorical teaching point; past perfect for prior effort ("had actually worked harder"); conditional reasoning about praise consequences ("if being smart explains success, then..."); reported counselor advice nested in dialogue; extended time sequencing across several months.

"You got every single question right! You're so smart!" Hana's mother exclaimed, looking over her graded math quiz with delight. Hana smiled, but something about the comment felt slightly off, though she couldn't quite explain why. The following week, a much harder quiz came home with several mistakes marked in red pen. Hana had actually worked harder on this one, staying up an extra twenty minutes studying multiplication tables she found genuinely difficult. "Oh," said her mother, her enthusiasm noticeably dimmer this time. "A few mistakes here." Hana's chest tightened. "I actually studied way more for this one than the easy quiz last week." Her mother paused, considering this. "You're right. That doesn't quite match up, does it? You worked harder this time but got a worse grade, and I responded like the opposite happened." That evening, Hana's mother mentioned the conversation to Hana's aunt, who worked as a school counselor. "I don't know why my reaction felt wrong to Hana," she admitted, "but she seemed a little hurt by it." "Can I share something I tell parents fairly often?" said the aunt. "When you praise 'You're so smart,' you're praising a fixed trait, something Hana either has or doesn't have. When you praise effort instead, like 'You worked so hard on that,' you're praising something she actually controls and can repeat." "But the smart comment was a compliment," said Hana's mother, a little defensive. "It is," agreed the aunt, "but it accidentally teaches something extra along with it. If being 'smart' explains success, then a bad grade might feel like proof she's actually not smart after all, even when she worked incredibly hard. The praise sets up failure to feel like an identity problem, not just a hard quiz." Hana's mother considered this carefully. "So what should I say instead?" "Try noticing the process," suggested the aunt. "Something like, 'I noticed you studied for twenty extra minutes this week. That really shows in how you approached the harder questions,' regardless of the actual grade." The next quiz that came home showed a middling grade, better than the difficult one but not perfect. "I noticed you made yourself a study schedule this time," said Hana's mother, trying the new approach carefully. "You stuck with it even on the day you had swim practice too." Hana looked up, surprised. "You noticed the schedule?" "I did," said her mother. "That's genuinely impressive planning, regardless of exactly what grade came back." Something in Hana's shoulders relaxed slightly. "I was actually worried you'd be disappointed about the grade." "I care much more about the studying you did than the specific number at the top of the page," said her mother. "The studying is something you control completely. The exact grade depends on lots of things, including how tricky the questions happen to be." Over the following months, Hana noticed her mother's comments shifting steadily-over-time toward the process — the studying, the planning, the persistence through hard problems — rather than praising results alone. "I don't feel as scared of quizzes anymore," Hana told her mother one evening. "Even when I get some wrong, it doesn't feel like it means something bad about me." "That's exactly the point," said her mother, smiling. "Your effort was always the actual achievement. The grade was just one small piece of evidence, not the whole story."
