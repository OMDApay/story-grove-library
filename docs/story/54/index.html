The Broken Window

The window was broken during recess, and nobody knew exactly how it had happened.

A little English

Passive voice used systematically throughout as the central grammar teaching point ("was broken," "was installed," "was posted," "were made"); past perfect for prior state ("had been learned"); reported dialogue with community-focused language; question tags for gentle confirmation ("It was already broken, wasn't it?").

The window was broken during recess, and nobody knew exactly how it had happened. "I didn't do it," said Ahmed, when the teacher asked the class."I didn't see anything," said Sara."It was already broken when I came back inside," said Omar. Mr. Hassan, the teacher, looked at the shattered glass carefully. "The window was broken sometime between 10:15 and 10:30, when you were all outside. Does anyone remember anything unusual?" The class was quiet. Nobody wanted to be the one who might get someone else in trouble. "Here's what I know," said Mr. Hassan. "The window wasn't broken before recess. It was broken during recess. And now it needs to be fixed before anyone gets hurt." He paused, looking around the room. "I'm not asking who broke it. I'm asking if anyone saw something that would help us figure out how to prevent it from happening again." Sami, who had been sitting quietly in the back, raised his hand slowly. "I saw something," he admitted. "But I don't want to get anyone in trouble." "Trouble isn't the goal here," said Mr. Hassan. "Safety is. What did you see?" "A ball was thrown," said Sami. "It hit the window. The ball was kicked too hard, and it flew toward the building instead of staying on the field." "Thank you for telling me," said Mr. Hassan. "That's really helpful information. Now we know: balls need to stay on the field, not toward the building." The following day, a new window was installed, and a sign was posted near the field: "BALLS MUST STAY ON THE FIELD." Nobody was punished. Nobody was blamed. But something important had been learned: when mistakes were made, the focus was on what could be done to prevent them next time, not on who had made them. That evening, Sami told his father about the whole situation. "I was worried Ahmed would be mad at me for telling," he explained. "But he wasn't. He said he was actually glad the rule was clear now, so nobody else would break a window inadvertently." "That's exactly right," said his father. "When something was broken, the important thing wasn't who broke it. It was making sure it wouldn't happen again. That's how communities stay safe." Sami considered this. "So being honest about what you saw isn't getting someone in trouble. It's helping everyone learn." "Exactly," said his father. "The window was fixed. The rule was posted. And now everyone knows what to do differently next time. That's what matters." The following week, during another recess, Sami noticed a younger student about to kick a ball toward the building. "Wait!" he called out. "The ball has to stay on the field. Remember the sign?" The younger student looked at the sign, then back at Sami. "Oh, I didn't see that. Thanks." Sami smiled. "No problem. That's what the rule was made for — so nobody gets hurt and no more windows get broken." From the classroom window, Mr. Hassan watched the whole exchange silently. The passive voice lesson hadn't been explicitly taught — it had been lived. And that, he knew, was how real learning happened.
