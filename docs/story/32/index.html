The Golden-Striped Football

"I want the new football with the golden stripes," Marwan announced, pointing at the shop window display.

A little English

Conditional planning structure ("if Marwan washed... he could earn"); present perfect for accumulated savings ("you'd have earned"); comparative reasoning between two purchase options; sequencing across multiple weeks; dialogue modeling financial decision-making through guided questions rather than direct instruction.

"I want the new football with the golden stripes," Marwan announced, pointing at the shop window display. "Can you buy it today?" His father studied the price tag. "That one costs eighty riyals. Do you have that much saved?" Marwan checked the small tin box where he kept his money. "I have twenty-three riyals." "So we have two choices," said his father. "I could just buy it for you right now, or you could earn the rest yourself and buy it with your own money. Which sounds more interesting to you?" Marwan considered this. "How would I earn fifty-seven more riyals?" "There are small jobs around the house," said his father. "Washing the car earns five riyals. Organizing the garage earns ten. Helping your grandmother with her grocery bags on Fridays earns eight." "That'll take forever," groaned Marwan. "Maybe," said his father. "Or maybe not as long as you think. Want to make a plan together?" They sat down and calculated: if Marwan washed the car twice a week and helped his grandmother every Friday, he could earn the remaining fifty-seven riyals in about five weeks. "Five weeks feels really long," said Marwan, deflated. "It does at the start," agreed his father. "But here's something interesting. Right now, that ball feels like the most important thing in the world. Let's see if that's still true in five weeks." Marwan started the following Saturday, scrubbing the car until his arms ached, earning his first five riyals. He added it to the tin box, watching the total climb slowly: twenty-eight, then thirty-six after organizing the garage, then forty-four after his first Friday with his grandmother's groceries. Halfway through the third week, Marwan noticed something at the sports shop — a different ball, plain black and white, no golden stripes, priced at only thirty-five riyals. "That one's actually the same brand," Marwan told his father, studying it closely. "It just doesn't have the fancy design." "Does the design matter for how well it bounces or kicks?" asked his father. Marwan thought about it. "Probably not. It's the exact same ball inside." "So which one do you actually want now?" asked his father. "The expensive one you originally saw, or this one, which would let you keep some of your earned money?" Marwan considered both options carefully. "If I get the cheaper one, how much would I have left over?" "You'd have earned forty-four riyals plus your original twenty-three," said his father. "That's sixty-seven total. The plain ball costs thirty-five. You'd have thirty-two riyals left." "I could save that for something else," said Marwan, his eyes lighting up. "Or even buy two smaller things instead of one big thing." They bought the plain black and white ball together that weekend, and Marwan proudly counted out his own earned money at the register, watching the cashier place his thirty-two riyals of change back into his hand. That evening, Marwan lined up his remaining money on his desk, already planning his next goal. "Next time I want something," he told his father, "I'm going to check if there's a version that costs less first, before deciding what I really want." His father smiled. "That single habit will save you more money over your whole life than almost anything else I could teach you."
