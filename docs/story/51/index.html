The Pink Sweater

Aunt Nour had brought a gift for every cousin, and when Rana opened hers, she found a bright pink sweater with sparkly buttons.

A little English

Second conditional for hypothetical reflection ("if I'd said the truth, she would've felt bad"); passive voice intro ("it was given"); reported advice dialogue with nuanced distinction between kindness and honesty; question tags for gentle confirmation ("You didn't actually like it, did you?").

Aunt Nour had brought a gift for every cousin, and when Rana opened hers, she found a bright pink sweater with sparkly buttons. "Do you like it?" asked Aunt Nour, radiant with anticipation. Rana lingered. She hated pink. She'd always hated pink. But she could see how excited her aunt looked, waiting for an answer. "It's... nice," said Rana, forcing a small smile. Her mother, sitting nearby, caught her eye briefly but said nothing. Later that evening, after the guests had left, Rana's mother found her folding the sweater carefully and putting it in the back of her drawer. "You didn't actually like it, did you?" asked her mother gently. Rana paused. "If I'd said the truth, she would've felt bad. So I just said it was nice." "That's called a white lie," said her mother. "And sometimes people do it to be kind. But I want you to think about something: what if the truth, said kindly, is actually better than a lie that sounds nice?" "How would that even work?" asked Rana. "Like, 'Thanks, but I hate this color'?" Her mother laughed. "Not exactly like that. But maybe, 'Thank you so much for thinking of me. I actually prefer blue, but I really appreciate the gift.' That's still kind, but it's also honest." Rana considered this. "But wouldn't that still hurt her feelings a little?" "Maybe," admitted her mother. "But here's what happens when we always say nice things we don't actually mean: people stop knowing what we really think. And then they can't actually give us things we'd like next time." "That makes sense," said Rana slowly. "So Aunt Nour might buy me another pink thing next year because she thinks I liked this one." "Exactly," said her mother. "Honesty, said gently, helps people learn what we actually prefer. Lies, even polite ones, keep everyone guessing." The following week, Rana's birthday came around, and Aunt Nour asked, "Any hints about what you'd like this year?" Rana took a breath. "I actually love blue more than pink. But I really appreciated the sweater you gave me last time." Aunt Nour smiled. "Blue, got it. Thank you for telling me." When the gift arrived — a blue jacket with silver zippers — Rana's face lit up wholeheartedly. "This is perfect!" she said, and this time, she meant it completely. Her mother, watching from the couch, gave her a small approving nod. The truth, it turned out, had worked better than the polite lie would have. That evening, Rana told her father about the whole situation. "I was worried Aunt Nour would be upset if I told her I prefer blue," she explained. "But she was actually happy I told her." "That's usually what happens," said her father. "Most people would rather know the truth so they can do better next time, instead of being told something nice that isn't actually true." "Can I ask you something?" said Rana. "Have you ever told a white lie to someone?" Her father thought about it. "Probably. Sometimes it feels easier in the moment. But I've noticed that when I do tell the truth kindly, even about something small, people actually trust me more over time. They know I'm not just saying whatever sounds nice." Rana considered this. "So being honest doesn't mean being rude?" "Not at all," said her father. "You can be honest and kind at the same time. It just takes a little more thought than saying whatever comes to mind."
