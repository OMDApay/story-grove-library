The Letter S

Every kid in Miss Salma's class could already write the letter S perfectly, except Yaseen.

A little English

Past perfect for backstory ("had improved," "didn't always"); comparative structure extensively used (better, smoother, more than); reported advice within dialogue (nested father-to-Reem-to-Yaseen wisdom chain); present perfect for accumulated change ("has improved so much").

Every kid in Miss Salma's class could already write the letter S perfectly, except Yaseen. His S always came out looking more like a wobbly worm than a proper letter, no matter how many times he tried. During handwriting practice, he crumpled his fourth attempt into a tight ball and shoved it to the bottom of his desk. "I'm just bad at writing," Yaseen announced to his tablemate, Reem. "Some people are good at it. I'm not one of them." Reem looked up from her own neat row of S's. "My mom says nobody's born knowing how to write. You just practice until your hand learns it." "Easy for you to say," Yaseen mumbled. "Yours already look perfect." "They didn't always," said Reem. "Want to see my old notebook from last year? I still have it at home." The next day, Reem brought her old notebook, and Yaseen stared in disbelief at the very first pages. Reem's early letters were crooked, uneven, some backward, nothing like her current handwriting at all. "This is really yours?" Yaseen asked, flipping through page after page of gradually improving handwriting. "Every single page," said Reem. "I used to think I just wasn't a 'handwriting person.' Then my dad told me something. He said talent is just what practice looks like after enough time has passed. Nobody starts good. They just don't show you the messy beginning part." Yaseen thought about this the whole way home. That evening, instead of avoiding his handwriting workbook like usual, he opened it and tried the letter S ten more times, comparing each attempt carefully to the one before it. The first three still looked wobbly. But by the seventh attempt, something shifted slightly — the curve came out a little smoother than before. He showed his father the row of ten S's, pointing at the difference between the first and the last. "Look, Baba. This one's still not perfect, but it's way better than this one." His father examined both letters closely. "You're right. That's real improvement, and you can see it because you kept trying instead of stopping after the first messy one." Over the following weeks, Yaseen kept a small "practice page" folded inside his backpack, adding a few letters every evening, comparing his newest attempts to his oldest ones rather than to anyone else's handwriting. A month later, during a class writing assignment, Miss Salma paused at Yaseen's desk, studying his neatly formed letters. "Yaseen, your handwriting has improved so much this month," she said, surprised. "What changed?" "I stopped comparing myself to Reem's handwriting," said Yaseen, "and started comparing myself to my own handwriting from last week instead. That's the only comparison that actually helped." Miss Salma smiled. "That might be the smartest thing I've heard a student say all year." That evening, Yaseen proudly showed his parents his full practice notebook, page after page of steadily improving letters. "I'm not someone who's bad at writing," he said firmly. "I'm someone who's still practicing. That's completely different."
