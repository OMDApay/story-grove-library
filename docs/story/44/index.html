The Cartwheel

"I can't do a cartwheel. I'm just not flexible like the other girls," Dania announced, flopping dramatically onto the grass after her fifth failed attempt at gymnastics…

A little English

Modal "can't" contrasted directly with modified form ("can't...yet") as the central grammar teaching point; conditional structure ("what if we changed"); present perfect for accumulated small improvements; direct quoted self-talk examples; reflective closing dialogue generalizing the technique beyond the original context.

"I can't do a cartwheel. I'm just not flexible like the other girls," Dania announced, flopping dramatically onto the grass after her fifth failed attempt at gymnastics club. Her coach, Miss Reem, walked over and sat down beside her. "Can I point something out? You just said 'I can't' three different times this week alone." "Because I can't," said Dania. "My legs go everywhere except where they're supposed to." "What if we changed one small word in that sentence?" suggested Miss Reem. "Instead of 'I can't do a cartwheel,' try saying 'I can't do a cartwheel yet.'" Dania frowned. "Does adding one word actually change anything?" "Try saying both sentences out loud and see how each one feels," said Miss Reem. Dania said the first version. "I can't do a cartwheel." It felt heavy, final, like a locked door. Then she tried the second version. "I can't do a cartwheel yet." Something about it felt different, lighter somehow, like the door was merely closed rather than locked forever. "That's strange," said Dania. "The second one doesn't feel as bad." "That's because 'I can't' sounds permanent, like it will always be true," said Miss Reem. "But 'not yet' means it's just true for right now, today, this specific moment. Tomorrow, or next month, it might not be true anymore." "But what if it's still not true even after I practice a lot?" asked Dania. "Then it becomes 'not yet' for a little longer," said Miss Reem simply. "The words don't promise you'll definitely succeed by any specific date. They just remind you that today's failure isn't the end of the whole story." Dania considered this. "Can I try again? With the 'yet' version in my head this time?" "Absolutely," said Miss Reem. Dania attempted the cartwheel again, teetering badly and landing on her side in the grass, laughing despite herself. "Still not yet," she said, sounding almost cheerful about it. Over the following weeks, whenever Dania felt the old "I can't" creeping into her thoughts, she deliberately added the word "yet" onto the end, even when it felt a little silly at first. "I can't land the cartwheel smoothly yet.""I can't keep my legs straight yet.""I can't stick the landing yet." Slowly, almost without her fully noticing, small pieces started improving. Her legs stayed straighter. Her landing became less wobbly. The changes were tiny at first, barely noticeable day to day. Six weeks after that first frustrated afternoon on the grass, Dania performed a clean, complete cartwheel in front of the whole gymnastics club, landing steady-now on both feet. "I DID IT!" she shouted, practically vibrating with excitement. Miss Reem smiled proudly. "Remember what you said six weeks ago?" "I can't do a cartwheel," Dania recalled. "But I guess it was actually just 'not yet' the whole time." That evening, Dania told her father about the cartwheel and the single word that had changed how the whole thing felt. "Now when something's hard," she said, "I just add 'yet' to it in my head, even for stuff way harder than cartwheels." Her father smiled. "That might be one of the most useful words you'll ever learn."
