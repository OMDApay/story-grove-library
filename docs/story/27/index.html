The Bouncing Leg

Miss Hanan noticed it every single day during story time: Tamer's leg bounced up and down under his desk, fast and constant, like a tiny engine that never turned off.

A little English

Present continuous for ongoing observed behavior ("is bouncing"); reported internal experience through dialogue Q&A; present perfect for teacher's ongoing observation ("I've been curious"); explanatory present tense for factual concept ("it's called self-regulation"); resolution dialogue between teacher and parent.

Miss Hanan noticed it every single day during story time: Tamer's leg bounced up and down under his desk, fast and constant, like a tiny engine that never turned off. "Tamer, can you please sit still?" Miss Hanan asked gently, for what felt like the hundredth time that week. Tamer froze his leg immediately, looking embarrassed. "Sorry, Miss Hanan." Within thirty seconds, the bouncing started again, without Tamer even seeming to notice. After class, Tamer stayed behind, looking worried. "Am I in trouble? I really am trying to stop." Miss Hanan sat down across from him, softening her expression. "You're not in trouble at all. Actually, I've been curious about something. Does your leg bouncing feel like something you choose to do, or does it just happen?" Tamer thought about this seriously. "It just happens. Especially when I'm sitting for a long time, or when the story gets really exciting. I don't even notice until you say something." "Does it feel bad when you do it?" asked Miss Hanan. "No," said Tamer, surprised by the question. "Actually, it kind of helps me listen better. If I try really hard to keep my leg totally still, I start thinking about my leg instead of the story." Miss Hanan nodded slowly. "That's really interesting, and it makes a lot of sense. Some people's bodies need a little bit of movement to help their brains focus, especially during quiet activities like listening to a long story. It's called self-regulation, and lots of people do it their whole lives, not just kids." "Grown-ups do it too?" asked Tamer, surprised. "My own father taps his pen constantly during long meetings," said Miss Hanan, smiling. "It drives my mother a little crazy, but it genuinely helps him think. It's just how some brains work best." "So I'm not being bad?" asked Tamer. "Not at all," said Miss Hanan. "But I do want to find a way for your leg to move without it bothering the students sitting near you, since some of them mentioned the bumping against their chairs. Would you like to try something?" The next day, Miss Hanan gave Tamer a small, quiet fidget object to hold during story time, and moved his seat slightly, away from the desks it had been knocking against. During the story, Tamer squeezed the fidget object rhythmically instead of bouncing his leg against the desk. His body still moved a little, but nothing bumped or distracted anyone else nearby. "How did that feel?" Miss Hanan asked afterward. "Really good, actually," said Tamer. "I remembered way more of the story today than usual." Over the following weeks, Miss Hanan noticed Tamer participating more during discussions, raising his hand more often, seeming generally more relaxed during quiet lesson time. She mentioned it to Tamer's mother during a parent meeting. "He's not misbehaving," Miss Hanan explained. "His body just needs a small outlet for movement while his brain focuses. Once we found the right outlet, everything else improved." Tamer's mother looked relieved. "I always worried something was wrong with him. I'm glad it was actually this simple." "His brain works differently, not wrongly," said Miss Hanan. "Once we stopped fighting the movement and found a quiet way to allow it instead, he could finally focus on what actually mattered — the story itself."
