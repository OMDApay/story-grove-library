The Shot Nobody Expected

"You throw like a girl," Jad said, laughing along with two other boys near the basketball hoop.

"You throw like a girl," Jad said, laughing along with two other boys near the basketball hoop. Mona froze, the basketball still warm in her hands from her last shot — a shot that had actually gone in, cleanly, through the net. "That doesn't even make sense," said Mona, though her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "I just scored." "It was probably lucky," said Jad, shrugging. "Girls aren't usually good at basketball. Everyone knows that." The other boys snickered. Mona felt her cheeks burn. She wanted to argue, but the words seemed to tangle in her throat. She walked home slower than usual that afternoon, replaying Jad's words over and over. By dinnertime, she still hadn't said much to anyone. Her older brother, Zaid, who played on the high school varsity team, noticed she seemed unusually quiet. "Rough day?" Mona explained what Jad had said at the court. Zaid set down his fork. "Do you know who Aisha Buhulaigah is?" Mona shook her head. "She's one of the fastest sprinters representing Saudi Arabia internationally," said Zaid. "And do you know who currently holds several of the most impressive all-around records in women's basketball history? Or how many incredible female athletes compete at the highest levels in the Olympics, in football, tennis, and nearly every sport you can imagine?" "I didn't know any of that," admitted Mona. "Jad's comment wasn't really about basketball," said Zaid. "It was an old, lazy idea that some people repeat without ever checking if it's true. The fastest way to prove an idea wrong isn't arguing about it. It's showing up and playing." The next Saturday, the neighborhood held its annual three-on-three tournament. Mona signed up, even though nervous butterflies filled her stomach the entire morning. Jad's team ended up playing directly against Mona's team in the second round. Whenever Mona had the ball, Jad guarded her loosely, clearly not expecting much of a challenge. But Mona had spent the whole week practicing her shot in the driveway with Zaid, working on exactly the kind of quick release Jad wouldn't expect. With the score tied late in the game, Mona received a pass near the three-point line. Jad lunged toward her, a half-second too late. Mona released the ball in one smooth motion. It arced through the air and dropped cleanly through the net. Her team erupted into cheers. Jad stood frozen, staring at the scoreboard. After the game, Jad walked over slowly, hands in his pockets. "That shot was... actually really good." "Thanks," said Mona simply, without gloating. "I guess I shouldn't have said what I said before," Jad admitted, looking embarrassed. "That wasn't fair of me." "No," agreed Mona. "It wasn't. But thanks for saying so now." That evening, Mona told Zaid about the game, the shot, and Jad's apology. "You didn't just win a game today," said Zaid, smiling. "You changed how someone thinks. That matters a lot more than the score."
