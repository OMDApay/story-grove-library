One Line, Four Days

A little English

Conditional worry structure ("What if I forget... what if everyone laughs"); present perfect for ongoing experience ("I still get nervous... even after doing this for three years"); sequencing markers (first, second, third) for coping strategy steps; past perfect brief reference; reflective past-tense narration of the climactic event.

In exactly four days, Yousra had to stand on a stage in front of the entire school and say her line in the class play, and the thought made her stomach hurt every single time she remembered it. "I only have one line," she told her older brother, Kareem, pacing back and forth in her room. "But what if I forget it? What if everyone laughs? What if my voice comes out squeaky?" Kareem, who performed in his high school band concerts regularly, set down his guitar. "Can I tell you a secret? I still get nervous before every single concert, even after doing this for three years." Yousra stopped pacing. "You do? But you always look so calm up there." "Looking calm and feeling calm are two completely different things," said Kareem. "My hands actually shake sometimes before I walk on stage. Everyone feels some version of that nervous feeling. Even professional actors talk about it." "So it never goes away?" asked Yousra, sounding disappointed. "It gets more manageable," said Kareem. "Want me to show you a trick that helps me?" Yousra nodded eagerly. "First," said Kareem, "practice your line so many times that your mouth could say it even if your brain went completely blank. Repetition builds a kind of backup memory in your body." That evening, Yousra said her line — "The garden needs water, or nothing will grow!" — exactly thirty times while brushing her teeth, folding laundry, and lying in bed waiting to fall asleep. "Second," Kareem continued the next day, "right before you go on, take three slow breaths, in through your nose, out through your mouth. It tells your nervous body that you're actually safe, even though it feels scary." Yousra practiced the breathing trick during dinner, in the car, and right before bed each night that week, until it started feeling instinctive. "Third," said Kareem on the morning of the play itself, "find one friendly face in the audience before you start. Look at them, not at everyone else. It makes the whole audience feel smaller and less scary." That evening, backstage, Yousra's stomach twisted into painful knots as she waited in her cardboard sunflower costume. Her hands felt cold and clammy. She took three slow breaths, just like Kareem had shown her. When her moment finally came, Yousra walked to the center of the stage, scanned the audience quickly, and found her mother's face in the third row, smiling encouragingly. She looked directly at her mother and said her line clearly: "The garden needs water, or nothing will grow!" The audience clapped. Yousra walked offstage, her heart still pounding, but with a huge grin spreading across her face. "I did it," she whispered to herself, almost in astonishment. "I actually did it." Backstage, Kareem was waiting with a proud smile. "How did it feel?" "Terrifying, right up until the exact second I said it," Yousra admitted. "Then it was over so fast, and I felt amazing." "That's exactly how it works, every single time, for pretty much everyone," said Kareem. "The fear right before is almost always worse than the actual moment." That night, Yousra told her parents she wanted to try out for a bigger role in next year's play. "A bigger part means more lines," she said, "but now I actually know how to get ready for the scary feeling instead of just hoping it goes away."
